# The Thinking Method — Condensed Guidebook

> Condensed from `thinking-method.md` (Language Transfer's *Thinking Method Guidebook for Course Writers*, 432 pp). Every concept is kept; prose, anecdotes and repetitions are compressed. German-specific material is marked **[DE]**; other-language examples are kept only where they teach a transferable mechanism (Arabic examples are kept as **[AR]** where they illustrate a general idea).

## 0. Introduction

### 0.1 The Guidebook
- **Purpose:** train new Language Transfer (LT) course writers; it is a first edition, deliberately unsimplified.
- **What it teaches:** not a template, format, or task list — a way of *thinking* about and reconstructing ideas. Base language (language of instruction) and target language dictate everything, so no fixed recipe can exist.
- **Why not "show":** teaching by showing content is like teaching hunting with the carcass instead of the chase. Animal mirror neurons help with physical actions, not mental processes. Content alone ≠ method.
- **How to read:** don't memorise; *think* as you read — constantly ask "what does this mean for the language I want to teach?"
- **Reality of course writing:** constant trial and error — write, test, edit, discover new mechanisms, rewrite. No secret template. The difficulty is front-loaded for the writer so the learner's ride is smooth.
- **Works in progress:** even the author redoes early classes endlessly; pages of material accumulate while the first classes are perfected. Finding problems on re-read = the process working.
- **Training path:** experience LT courses as a learner while analysing as a teacher; read the guidebook once while analysing courses, again during training.

### 0.2 The Ghost of Michel Thomas
- The Thinking Method is **not** "just the Michel Thomas (MT) Method," though it shares the Socratic Q&A format.
- **What was borrowed/learned from MT:** the freedom inside Q&A (any content order, any explanation). Cognate conversions were "cool" but not the inspiration.
- **What is original:** the complex, interwoven course structures are products of the author's own teaching experiments; the Socratic approach alone can produce chaos as easily as clarity.
- **Legal history:** Hodder & Stoughton (MT's publisher) sent a cease-and-desist citing a patent on *the pause button*; the EFF defended LT pro bono; the absurdity made international news. The patent never described the MT method itself, so no methodological debate was possible.
- **Naming:** "Thinking Method" is an ironic jab at how little thought goes into teaching/learning; "Language Transfer" alludes both to vocabulary transfer and to the linguistic term for (often erroneous) transfer between languages — language transfer as the mechanism by which languages breed and change. Negative transfer must be countered in teaching, but is also how languages evolve.

### 0.3 What is Language?
- **Thought ≠ language.** Language is a *transcription* of (some of) thought. "What language do you think in?" really means "what language do you use when you dialogue with yourself?"
- Evidence: the deaf don't think exclusively in signs; language-less humans ("wolf-children", unexposed deaf children) and animals still think, calculate, decide.
- **Language is magic:** a "spell" and "to spell" are the same word; language transmits complex thought via sound/symbols by shared convention.
- **Language = words/sounds + structure (word order).** "I can" vs "can I" shows order carries meaning. Many important sounds are not words. Definition: *a shared imagination about the meanings carried in sounds and the order we put them in, used to express mental events.*
- **Consequence for teaching:** transcribe *thought* into the target language, not the base language into the target language. If learners translate forms directly, they lose clarity, memorise instead of understand, and think about things they don't need.
- **Multidimensional thought vs linear language:** an idea (e.g. "he ran to the park really fast on Tuesday because he was racing with his dog") happens all at once; language forces sequence ("which part comes first?"). Much course work = separating an idea into elements, then encoding one bit at a time in the target language.
  - Swahili "I already gave him it": past + giving are separate elements; "it" is not represented (Swahili doesn't represent two objects this way). Shows how brutal form-transfer would be vs. idea-transcription.
  - German likewise demands splitting ideas because its information order differs greatly from English. **[DE]**
- **Languages overlap at the macro level** (all have present/past/future, singular/plural nouns, adjectives), but each slices reality differently.
- **Forms ≠ thoughts:** Spanish *ser/estar* — *está rojo* (state: glowing metal) vs *es rojo* (characteristic: painted metal); "I am married" = *estoy casado* or *soy casado* depending on dialect. English "you're coming" covers present and future; Spanish *estás viniendo* is only now, but *vienes más tarde* conveys the same future thought with a different form. We say the same thing while literally saying something different.
- **Dialects vs languages is political:** a "language" is a dialect promoted for administrative/national unity; Basque is a "tongue" unrelated to Spanish; French/Portuguese/Romanian/Catalan are essentially Latin dialects, just as Moroccan/Tunisian/Egyptian are "Arabic." There are only dialects; promotion is political.
- **Teaching implication:** the clearer the teacher is about what language is, the more learners naturally absorb it and treat language as a tool rather than an impulse.
- **Courses are imperfect:** made under resource/pressure constraints, sometimes without native-speaker input or conversation in the target language; new writers should find faults and surpass the author, not imitate him.

## 1. The Ten Things We Do in the Thinking Method

- Not a fixed format or rule set: each moment marries considerations of language, teaching and common sense; the writer decides what goes where and why. The book teaches *how to think*, not procedure.
- The method developed organically; dissecting it into parts is artificial. A moment may serve several ideas at once or be two contradictory ideas fighting. **No one-size-fits-all.** The method is extremely language-specific in both target and base language.

### 1.1 We Inhabit the Learner's Mental Theatre
- Constantly relive the learner's experience. **Never assume knowledge** — experts forget what is not obvious; e.g. musicians say "chord" to laypeople, who then equate chord with note. Misinformation breeds where we assume.
- Don't assume the learner knows noun/verb, nor that a target-language behaviour is "obvious." Example: Spanish pronoun placement (*comí un bocadillo* → *lo comí*), unnoticed until the learner produces \**comí lo*. An imaginary learner in your head catches such gaps before testing.
- **The learner knows only what you have told them, when you told them.** Keep shaping reality around what has been taught so far. This is the "panorama": the writer sees the whole language; the learner sees only a partially constructed panorama.
- **Cognitive skills, not just knowledge:** we teach mental processes step by step; track 5 ≠ track 50 in cognitive demand. Don't assume the learner has the required skill either.
- **Editing danger:** blurring what *you* know vs what the *learner* knows (after moving material) causes careless, sometimes fatal structural mistakes. Each full revision needs a fresh mental theatre.
- **Inhabiting makes thought-transcription intuitive:** it only *looks* like translating words/structures; really we transcribe the thought already encoded in the base language into the target language.
- Example: English present "I come" vs Spanish *vengo* — form correspondence but not thought correspondence (*vengo* = "I am coming"; "I come now" is wrong English). Focus on thoughts prevents tripping over form/use mismatches. Know the base/target correspondences before writing, and let the mental theatre catch what you missed.
- **Needs experience:** an activated learner from studying an LT course helps; live practice runs are essential — learners react remarkably similarly, and every practice run feeds the mental theatre.
- **Stop when you leave the theatre:** writing while no longer inhabiting the learner produces text you'll later struggle to fix; better to scrap and restart (same as telling learners to stop when no longer consciously engaged).
- **Motivation:** inhabiting the learner makes sessions enjoyable, productive, and keeps you aware of what you are creating.
- Every stage (writing, revising, practising, editing) asks: what is each utterance doing in the learner's mental theatre, and what trace does it leave?

### 1.2 We Teach Everything, One Thought at a Time
- **"Complete" course:** cover every structure that can arise in spoken language — not by visiting every structure, but by equipping the learner to construct any sentence themselves (vocabulary permitting).
- **One *thought* at a time**, not one form. What feels like one idea is often four or five. Writing then testing exposes the "gaping holes."
- **Complete Spanish present-tense example:** initial plan = strip infinitive ending, add *-o*. Missing elements: (a) the idea of the infinitive ("to" form) and how Spanish makes it; (b) word stress — infinitives stressed at the end (*hablar*), present on the penultimate syllable (*hablo*). Solution: pre-teach *quiero*, *intento* + converted infinitives (*explorar*), highlighting the "to" with intonation, so *-o* is digested in other contexts; then weave the present tense, introducing only the stress shift. Superficial focus on vocabulary conversion hides the build-up; the learner doesn't notice. Present tense therefore moves from the opening to much later.
- **Method for beginners:** list every separable idea/movement leading to the form; then ask which single one should come first. Usually obvious once listed.
- **Swahili negatives:** negation requires negative subject (*ha-*, which can mutate to *hu-*) and negative temporal infixes that are largely arbitrary (*-li-* → *-ku-*; *-me-* → *-ja-*; present *-na-* vanishes and final *-a* → *-e*). Path: learn *kuna* / *hakuna* ("there is/isn't"; *hakuna matata*) to digest *ha-* without a subject → apply *ha-* to subjects in the **negative future**, whose *-ta-* need not change (optional *-to-*), postponing arbitrary temporal changes. Languages generally offer such a pathway if you look.
- **Resist offloading:** the urge to dump everything at once is the *writer's* feeling, not the learner's. A well-designed course leaves the learner feeling complete at each step; only the writer feels the missing information.

### 1.3 We Manage Cognitive Load and Tension Contours
- We gauge load from the mental theatre and shape the course in response. Most material rises gradually; we want **contours** (peaks and troughs), not a gradient.
- **Artificial friction:** deliberately leaving things slightly harder than necessary to create tension and cue participation (stop and think!) — visible in Part 3 course openings.
- **Optimum cognitive load** is a home base: neither drowning (chin barely above water) nor so easy that the learner can succeed while thinking of something else.
- **Digestion metaphor:** troughs let the learner digest what was bitten off; peaks make them hungry for more. Flex/relax is a brain workout; create an engaging, memorable experience.
- **Load-lowering techniques:** change of subject, abandoning the current focus, masked repetition, an interesting language offshoot.
- **Overload = panic mode:** learner clutches key-seeming (often wrong) information, abandons all other thought, repeats a mantra ("*hablar*, to speak…"). Method impossible if the learner is half elsewhere. Their eyes betray the exit; holding a "mental object" produces an absent look; interference reveals the culprit (e.g. *comgo* for *como* because *tengo*'s *-go* wasn't released).
- Write for a **panic-mode-prone student**.
- **Under-load = boredom:** going too slowly makes learners leave mentally anyway. Both extremes lose the learner.

### 1.4 We Import Knowledge
- Nobody learns from scratch. Language is unique: everyone is already a language expert, but unconsciously (nobody is a "subconscious chemist").
- Import can be **unconscious (by omission — not reinventing the wheel)** or **conscious**.
- **Don't describe what the base language already does.** Methods that ignore the base language describe everything, forcing learners to decode grammar without noticing the target matches the base, or noticing too late. Superfluous description becomes **mental debris** that resurfaces and interferes.
- Example: "I want it" → *ich will es* does not need an explanation of subject–verb–object order. **[DE]**
- **Partial overlap:** leave some things unsaid, over-explain others. English "would": reference its use in context rather than define "conditional"; later handle divergence ("I would go every Saturday" = habitual, not conditional) and target-only meanings (Greek *tha éfaga* = "I was going to eat" as well as "I would eat" — teach the shared conditional sense first, then add the intention sense with vivid imaginary contexts).
- **Positive vs negative transfer:** start with what is the same (positive), then address what differs but risks being assumed the same (negative).
- **Conscious import examples:**
  - **Spanish subjunctive:** show English has one ("it is important that you be here" vs "are here"; "had I gone…"), demystifying it and reducing resistance. Import only the *idea*/feeling, not the mechanics (English uses infinitive/inversion; Spanish differs), and jump between English strategies to prevent premature conclusions about Spanish forms. Key insight prepared: the subjunctive often changes *feeling*, not meaning.
  - **Grammatical case (German, Greek)** **[DE]**: show English already handles case via she/her, he/him, who/whom; then *use* that knowledge as a test — substitute he/him, she/her, they/them to find the case ("can you clean she/her?" → *the kitchen*; "we baked it for they/them" → *the children*). English lacks case for "you"/"it", so develop with he/him and they/them.
  - **Importing even "misinformation":** English inversion in questions (Can I? / Do I want?) is exceptional in English but the norm in many languages; use the English exception to introduce target behaviour.
  - **Sounds:** "new" sounds are often familiar sounds behaving differently. Greek's two TH sounds = English "then"/"think," now split into two letters; Arabic's two S sounds exist in English as "see"/"saw"; Arabic breathy H exists rarely in English ("Harry") but can end a word (*raayeh*). Accent = approximations; importing sounds frees cognitive load.
  - **Vocabulary import (all current LT courses):** word endings, beginnings, consonant shifts, storytelling/onomatopoeia. The vocabulary is almost always *for something else*: Spanish *-al* words teach vowel sounds and stress; "cancelation → *cancelar*" highlights the infinitive as a launchpad for tenses; Greek *meno / perimeno / epimeno* (peri-meter, epi-sode) teaches word-building and word-splitting for long words. Free words are a by-product.
- **Direct translation = negative/reversed import:** "In Spanish you say 'I want that you go' rather than 'I want you to go'" — the wrongness in English *is* the information. Turkish: "I an ice cream want" (or more direct "I ice cream want") conveys SOV without teaching what subject/object mean. Be direct only about what you're highlighting (word order, not the article), which avoids side-effects. Vilified by traditional methods because of how wrong it looks in print — but it works *because* it sounds wrong.
  - *Gustar*: translate as "to be pleasing **to** me"; "I like them" = "they are pleasing to me"; "they like it" needs *les* ("to them") not *los*. The "Spanglish" step makes the indirect object intuitive without a single grammatical description.

### 1.5 We Reframe
- Reframing = describing a language's behaviour in a way that breaks with convention. Don't be alternative for its own sake, but don't inherit conventions unexamined either; notice when you're reproducing convention without choosing it.
- **Arabic roots [AR]:** conventionally pronounced with vowels (/sa/fa/ra/ for /s/f/r/), but Arabic = consonant roots × vowel patterns (*sifaara* = embassy). Pronouncing roots as bare consonants avoids interfering with the root/pattern model. A reframe only visible from the learner's mental theatre.
- **Mandarin stative verbs:** a book treated "is beautiful" as a stative verb; the author's notes questioned it (stative verb vs. omitted "to be," as in Arabic) without being able to decide yet — start thinking about alternatives immediately.
- **Cross-language awareness:** other languages' mechanisms (Arabic) enable hypotheses about a new one (Mandarin). Convention evolves in isolation, so the same term ("subjunctive") can mean different things per language, and identical behaviours can be described totally differently (Mandarin stative verbs ≈ Arabic absent "to be"). Don't submit easily to tradition, however old. Conventions are stubborn.

#### 1.5 (continued) More reframes
- **German word-order sandwich [DE]:** instead of 1,001 rules, base everything on one idea — German structure **avoids interruption**. Two verbs form a sandwich: one verb early, the other at the end, everything else inside: *Ich will morgen mit dir spielen* ("I want tomorrow with you to play"). The final verb is the key information, so the sentence must not be cut off before it. Variations fall out of the same idea:
  - Fronting/inversion (*Morgen will ich mit dir spielen*): to emphasize something else, you pull it out of the sandwich — and something must go back in (subject–verb inversion).
  - Subordinating conjunctions (*weil*): interruption risk rises when connecting clauses, so the second verb is pushed to the very end.
  - Note: the sandwich was designed with these future explanations in mind even before they were recorded.
  - **Phrasal verbs reframe:** *aufmachen* = *auf* + *machen*; *ich mache es auf* = "I open it." Instead of "I make it on" or an English-phrasal-verb comparison ("I'll look it up"), *auf* is reframed as "still a verb" (a verb bit), so separable verbs join the same sandwich rule rather than getting their own separate rule (and aren't confused with prepositions). *machen* + *auf/an/zu* → open / turn on / close: the last word carries the key meaning.
  - **Reverse engineering:** the inversion is taught first via the two-verb sandwich; once learned, its justification can be dropped and the inversion extended to one-verb clauses. Example of weaving.
- **Swahili noun groups [general]:** "it/they/them" reference sounds depend on noun class (≈10 "genders"), normally taught as arbitrary vocabulary. Reframed into coherence: *ki/vi* → *ki/vi*; *mu/mi* (M suppressed) → *u/i*; *n/n* → *i/zi*; *m/wa* → *a/wa*. Teach the coherent examples first (*wa* plural before the less coherent *m-* singular) to establish the hypothesis; treat N/no-prefix nouns' *i/zi* as the same "unspecific it/they" used for phantom subjects ("it's starting"). Remaining sounds become scattered vocabulary items. Result: a system plus exceptions instead of random data.
- **Turkish "case" reframed out [general]:** Turkish has no real case system — "from you" is "you-from," "to you" is "you-to." Only word formation matters; *burada* (in here), *buradan* (from here), *buraya* (to here) follow from raising consciousness that English "here" implies to/from/in. Avoids ablative-case middleman terminology.
- **English "do" reframe (English for Spanish Speakers):** rather than "add *do* to make a question," define *do* as **emphatic** (I *do* want; like spoken italics, not *sí*). Negatives and questions are inherently non-affirmative, so you first signal emphasis with *do*, then add *not* or invert. "Not" is the only negator across the language; inversion the only question marker; "do" the warning. "Do-phobic" verbs (can, must…) simply skip the warning step — they also lack infinitives ("don't like *do* or *to*"). *Did* carries past; past negatives/interrogatives therefore come before affirmative past forms (wanted, taught). Turns tens of rules into a few omnipresent ideas; only makes sense to the *sentence-builder*, i.e. from inside the mental theatre.
- **Progressive vs conservative balance:** if the language itself offers more coherence than tradition, your explanation is closer to the truth. But don't force ideas the language doesn't support — the target language has the final say, so keep listening to it. Mushroom parable: shunning conservatism can lead you to a new delicacy *or* to poison; both rigid traditionalism and innovation-for-its-own-sake fall short.

### 1.6 We Weave
- **Language behaves like a plot** — except the plot ignores linear time; teaching is linear, so course order itself provides context, foreshadowing and payoff.
- **Vertical vs horizontal:** preparation tables/charts/diagrams group related content (conjugations, agreement, phonetics) — useful for the writer, **not** the teaching order. Language never builds sentences from one vertical column alone; coherence often runs horizontally across word groups and behaviours.
- **Don't inherit textbook orders** (greetings first, then numbers/colours/weather) regardless of structural complexity.
  - *¿Cómo estás?* comes in Complete Spanish only when contrasting *ser/estar* with *¿cómo eres?*; *¿de dónde eres?* arrives when teaching that Spanish doesn't end in prepositions, and re-weaves *ser/estar* against *¿dónde estás?*. Delayed placement creates "wow moments" and resolves phrases learners have already half-heard (e.g. *¿cómo te llamas?* → "so that's why it's *te* not *tú*!").
  - Swahili "yes" (*ndiyo*, "indeed") comes very late: no rush; courses are 15–20 hours total, so frequency-based ordering is nearly irrelevant.
- **Course order is one of the teacher's most important tools.** Always pre-install the background so only one new element appears at a time; keep the overall destination in mind while exploring other areas.
  - Hence **no lesson index** exists for LT courses: a tense "covered" in tracks 23–26 has had its components pre-inserted across earlier tracks, with some features delayed to tracks 48–54 or 72–74.
- **Don't let superficial order dictate explanations** — explanations must dictate order. Think about any explanation in the context of all others (how they interact and support each other).
  - Writers (even the author) still dump a whole area onto one page first; revision then spreads it across fifteen pages, interwoven with past and future material.
- **Weaving opportunities appear during writing/editing**, sometimes by accident; stay open-minded and don't fight the plan — "hear" the language suggesting pathways.
- **Spanish demonstratives example:** *este/ese, estos/esos* look arbitrary (unlike *esta/estas*). Weave: relate **-e to *el*** and **-os to *los*** (and *-a/-as* to *la/las*), even though demonstratives never appear with articles — reduces "mental debris" and creates learner confidence ("can't wait to hear what new mysteries you will unlock"). Neuter *esto/eso* relate later to *lo/ello*. *Aquel/aquella* ("over there") kept separate (different forms, higher load, counterintuitive history from *acá/aquí*); if included, same article logic (*-el/-los*, *-la/-las*) after the double-L is comfortable.
  - Must not teach articles for the first time *and* expand into demonstratives in the same block — articles must already be dominated.
- **English demonstratives (hypothetical):** little rule-based logic (this/these, that/those), so weave memory support: historically English marks past by lengthening vowels (swim→swam, run→ran); other languages play with sound (Greek shifts stress back for past, Spanish forward for future). Then pose the question: are plural demonstratives lengthening vowels? Offered as a loose, interesting consideration (not a claim) — cross-referencing knowledge aids memory.
- **Swahili negative subjects:** first person saved for last though most frequent — because *ni* → *si* is the exception to *ha-* (not *hani*). Saving it strengthens the rule; and *si* fits "Swahili the great recycler" (*ku* = infinitive, negative past, "you" object, noun class; *ni* = I/me, is/are/am, "in"; *je* = question particle, "how"): even the negative of "to be" (*si*) mirrors the negative of "I." Coherence foreshadowed across the whole course.
- **German "the" and case [DE]:** reactivate the TH→D consonant shift (English "the" pronounced /thii/). *die* (fem./pl.) is learned first because of /thii/. Then *das* / *der* split into D- (from "the") + *es* (it) / *er* (he); *die* = D- + *sie* (she/they). Case follows free of charge: "to her" = *ihr*, so "to the lady" = *der Frau* — D- + *ihr*. Vowel shift *ihr*→*der* is unimportant (precedent: *das* ← *es*). Turns the case panorama into a walk in the park, from very early in the course. Complete Greek differs: it uses the pronouns directly as articles — comparing the two courses is a study in weaving.
- **German adjective endings (hypothetical) [DE]:** *der große Baum* vs *ein großer Baum*; *das große Haus* vs *ein großes Haus*. Insight: *-er* looks like *der*, *-es* like *das* — the adjective marks **definiteness** added by the adjective itself ("a big tree" is more specific than "a tree"); with a real definite article only *-e* remains, as a connector (weave into case). Pre-weave definiteness cues before the feature arrives; use a tension contour (references whose relevance the learner can't yet see) and resolve it at the adjectives.

### 1.7 We Mask Repetition
- Rote repetition is boring *and* makes the mind abandon conscious processes for shortcuts; internalisation is necessary for fluency, but overt repetition accelerates it prematurely, bypassing the conscious processes the course installs.
- **Shortcuts are simplified rules**: they work for the current sentences (hard for the teacher to detect) but fail in later applications — which is why the teacher didn't choose them.
- Repetition is constant in the Thinking Method but mostly invisible; it also occurs naturally through weaving.
- **Methods:**
  - **Change the wording:** "second-to-last syllable" then "penultimate syllable." Also cues non-literal understanding.
  - **Alternative explanations:** rarer, and must not interfere with other ideas. Arabic patterns [AR]: template ("1st root-*i*-2nd-*aa*-3rd-*a*") or a model word (*sifaara*) whose root you replace. Serves different learners and masks repetition.
  - **New sounds:** never drill in one go (mouth tenses). Abandon and return; describe the sound in many ways — what the mouth does/omits, how it feels, spelling, similarity to a base-language sound.
  - **Feedback on target sentences:** repeat the sentence for practical reasons (audibility, indirect correction) and use it to affirm processes — e.g. "you removed *-ar* (which means 'to'), added -O for 'I', and pulled the stress to the penultimate, second-to-last, syllable." Vary how much process you replay (sometimes one element, sometimes none) so feedback itself never becomes predictable.
  - **Vary target sentences:** change a verb/noun, make affirmative interrogative (even just by intonation), introduce/shift a contraction, contrast a similar form with different meaning — keep the same underlying process under a new surface.

### 1.8 We Cue
- From the very beginning, tell the learner what participation means: pause, think, answer aloud; don't obsess with remembering; relax; concentrate when needed; let go of the last item; trust you won't let them forget. **Think slowly to learn quickly.** No autopilot. Doubts will be resolved by periodic repetition; don't derail with irrelevant questions.
- Cue away bad habits: active memorisation, simplifying the teacher's rules, treating target sentences as **tests** rather than **exercises**.
- Cueing works on **two levels**: explicit (explanations/instructions) and implicit. It reduces cognitive load and declutters (much is implied, not said).
- **Cueing types of information:** key/essential vs supporting vs interesting. Non-key tangents (consciousness-raising, load resets) are cued by wording/intonation, consistently, so learners can distinguish them. Requires knowing exactly what each moment is doing.
- **Cueing aims:**
  - Not fluency now but the framework for fluency; *find* sentences every time, don't recite; sentences are not tests; imagine the learner in a live setting thinking their way through.
  - **Spoken language focus:** written-only points are cued as secondary. Spelling is used for other purposes (sound perception — P/B, hard G/K; grammar points; etymology), so first make sure the learner doesn't think they must learn spelling.
  - Grammar decisions matter more than pronunciation now; pronunciation improves through exposure later ("we'll improve this sound the more we hear it," "better!" then move on). Also resolves tension (unfamiliar sounds are frustrating).
- **Cueing implicit imports:** unless told otherwise, assume the target works like the base language. This must stay implicit — saying it would be taken too literally. Most visible with word order (English question-word fronting: "you go **there**" vs "**where** do you go?" — Arabic doesn't move it [AR]). Trust is built by never asking for a sentence the learner has no means to generate; if they overthink and over-modify, divide the sentence into smaller parts to preserve order.
- **Cueing submission (trust):** the learner must let the teacher control the process — not make their own rules, not worry about the unknown, not build vertical summaries from horizontal teaching. (Arabic /s/l/m/ links "submission" and "peace" [AR].) Show practically that the method is best; cues can be subtle to forceful; occasionally telling a learner they're not holding up their end is justified with "those students" who insist on their own rules. Exhaust all plausible cueing first.
- **Cueing for doing:** don't let learners fixate on literal instructions (memory mutates them; one wrong word re-teaches something false). Vary the wording ("get rid of the verb ending," "remove the final *-ar*," "take away the bit that means 'to'") so the *action* is the takeaway. Same technique as masking repetition but a different intent (often both at once).
- **Examples over instructions:** instructions often only make sense *after* the example; the first statement of an instruction is just the gel. Don't let a misunderstood instruction block learning — the learner waits for the example. What remains at the end is the **movement/manipulation**, not the corruptible verbal middleman.
- **Miscueing — avoid artificial mnemonics:** meaningless hooks (*tú* = "too, you too!") blur the line between meaningful justification and empty connection, and stop learners making their own discoveries. They imply everything needs a hook, so learners panic when something "just is" (or invent their own false reasons). Some things must be accepted as given.
  - Similar-looking features used for other purposes are *not* mnemonics: *kula* from *kulala* (accent movement), *kutaka/kutoka* (artificial friction to force conscious differentiation), "I see the sea"/"I can't hear you here" (English homophones — the *mixed* nature of English). Never "kucheka = 'to laugh', like *check* if you're laughing."
  - Mnemonics allowed only when stakes are very high and embedded in a real explanation: *haber* (/aber/) sourcing A and E for the two irregular present branches (*he, ha* / *hemos, has*), with terminations built by the learner and person-based logic still doing the explaining.

### 1.9 We Correct Correctly
- Anyone can say "wrong"; the teacher must know **what went wrong** in the learner's process — hence the mental theatre. Correction time is also used to mask repetition and weave in new material. Sometimes correct indirectly, or not at all.
- Don't assume the learner didn't think; that assumption demotivates. If unsure, ask for the sentence again "not from memory, but thinking it through again"; if it recurs, step them through the process — learners often blurt out the faulty reasoning. A skilled teacher can pluck the logical cause from thin air.
- **Guided self-correction example:** *He comida* → "Are we using an adjective here?" → *He comido!* → affirm that *comido* is a participle, not an adjective, and doesn't change for gender/number. Alternate diagnosis: *la comida* = "food" (recent noun) → mask repetition of "to eat." Which diagnosis fits depends on context (recent material, learner's gender, etc.).
  - Romance participles double as adjectives, so learners (and French/Italian habits) want to agree them — worth preventative correction even if the error was careless.
- **Learners may not hear what they actually said** — they can insist they said *comido* while saying *comida* repeatedly. Check the learner is aware of their own output.
- **Recording advantages:** editing lets you insert great post-hoc corrections (if the sound matches), and most carelessness corrections are cut; keeping some shows the recording student also errs (remote learners forget the audio is edited and feel inferior to the "genius").
- **Preventative correction:** address confusions before they occur — Romance participle/adjective homography; Swahili sound recycling (spotlight it so learners reinterpret rather than get stuck); *me gusto* vs *me gusta* for *gustar*.
- **Masked correction:** affirm correct processes — "good, you removed the last *-la* and pulled the accent back too!" — congratulating the right and silently correcting remote learners who erred. Creates the "teacher in the room" feeling and lets learners resolve doubts themselves.
- **Indirect correction:** not every error, especially pronunciation slips under cognitive load, can be addressed directly without breaking tension contours. Repeat the model sentence with intonation on the corrected bit ("well done, *I want* — them to go"); remote learners notice even if the recording student doesn't.
- **Prompted self-correction:** *Queren?* → *Quieren!* → "Great, when we stress down on that E of *quer-* it splits into IE!"
- Correction = fixing misunderstandings, flawed processes and bad interaction with the material, opportunistically combined with repetition masking.

### 1.10 We Increase Language and Learning Consciousness
- Learners carry culturally normalised false assumptions about language (especially that language = thought). These act as a contradictory background to your explanations. Address them.
- **Anecdote:** a Spanish student, weeks in, asked whether English "has rules like Spanish" — "In English you just speak!" He had imported knowledge so seamlessly that he never became conscious of English as a structural mechanism. Hence the pillar: **separate language from thought** explicitly and implicitly.
- People feel they *are* their language, as they feel they are their arm (intention → speech, like intention → reaching). Pry them apart:
  - Talk about "information being represented," not "words."
  - Direct translations show thought as multi-dimensional, not linear.
  - Rephrase the same target sentence with base-language synonyms ("I'll pick him up / collect him tomorrow") to show "pick up" is an *idea*, not a vocabulary item (only when cognitive load allows).
  - Optionally discuss it directly (load reset, reordering practice, tension play).
- **Base-language consciousness:** don't say "Spanish makes the plural like English" if the learner isn't conscious of English. Instead: "In English we add an S — *car, cars*. In Spanish we'll do the same!" But this is not a fixed rule: sometimes leaving it open serves tension (English "them" as unintuitive plural of "it" vs *los* = *lo* + S, which the learner can derive; plurals are conscious enough for that).
- **Learning-process consciousness:** describe and counter shortcuts learners make. *Ser/estar* = state/characteristic, **not** temporary/permanent — the tempting opposite pair is mostly true but wrong; use it to teach learners not to simplify your rules, pre-empting what their minds will do "in spite of themselves."
- **Bridge to the real world:** describe hypothetical encounters with the target language and the mental moves for extracting information from exposure after the course.
- Techniques combine freely: one sentence can weave, cue, reframe and preventatively correct at once.

## 2. Teaching

- A great script cannot survive bad teaching; a merely good script with great teaching beats a perfect script with faulty teaching. Teaching deserves almost as much emphasis as writing.

### 2.1 Knowing Your Script and Knowing Your Audience
- The mental theatre only becomes authentic through **many real classes with different people**; the more you teach, the more "voices" inhabit it.
- Learner types ≠ "visual/auditory"; what matters is **personality** and the emotional environment brought to formal learning — cautious, happy-go-lucky, obsessive, stubborn, uncertainty-intolerant (the last asks constant questions that pull the course vertical, e.g. demanding conjugations not yet taught).
- Courses must anticipate these tendencies, and teaching must handle them ad hoc. Know learners intimately; eventually you home straight in on their baggage.
- **Know the script cold.** Writing ≠ knowing it. Only repeated run-throughs give you spare cognitive load to watch delivery and reception. Every teacher must always know what is coming, what came before, and how the panorama is being pieced together.
- Writing experience and teaching income fit together: private students and open workshops are the natural testing ground.

### 2.2 The Performance
- Teaching is concentration- and energy-intensive. We ask learners to let go, trust, focus — that cannot be demanded, it must be **won**. A great course delivered like a shopping list won't be felt as great.
- **Dominate the space with energy.** When your energy drops, learners relapse into guessing/memorising — proof of energy's effect. High energy is essential early in the course and in high-load moments; it need not be sustainable all the time because learners must eventually self-motivate.
- **Buenos Aires anecdote:** *Porteño* rigidity/ego made classes a battle; belief in the method was superstitious ("believed enough to pay, not enough to change habits"). Brazilians flowed into the method; the tango/salsa scene (rigid columns vs flowing pendulums) illustrated cultural paradigms — negative "language transfer" of body habits. The first Brazilian couple physically relaxed at the word "relax"; Porteños got *more* rigid, worrying how to relax and whether they were relaxing — "isn't trying not to memorise, memorising?" Learners are often culturally typical; work around it.
- Solution: stop reading/instruction and start **performing**; energy raises what can be achieved in one class or tension contour, counters baggage, even wakes sleepy learners (better solved by timetable change or coffee/sweets).
- Performance captivates ("moths to light bulbs") and makes learners *want* to play ball. Energy must be **genuine**, not louder talk or a fake smile (which patronises). It comes through eyes, voice, body language, human connection; learners mirror investment and can enter the submissive state that lets them see the method work.
- Example: Cyprus Project 4-hour workshops — participants buzzing afterwards, "an event, not a class." Energy is audible even in recordings made under precarious, noisy, stressful conditions: genuine overall energy can be summoned as its shadow on demand.
- **Extreme example:** recorded Introduction to Turkish the day a close friend was killed by a hit-and-run; chose to step into the teaching mind, class was normal, and therapeutic — leaving yourself at the door can be healthy and makes the "submission" feel smaller. Energy can even convince *you*.
- **The teaching hat / teaching personality:** automatically gleeful, optimistic, generous, excitable, open, welcoming, motivated, expert, certain it will work and be pleasurable; connected to your problems yet separate; private despite being a "minor public figure" whom learners feel they know/own. Each teacher must find their own "head for the hat" (for the author: desperation to share what fascinates him). Nurture it.
- Performance reframe: like treating guitar recitals or exams as an opportunity to *show off* rather than as intimidating tests — nervous energy converts into bright performance energy. The Cyprus workshop entrance (shabby clothes, dog, borrowed bicycle; visible audience shock) became the first tension contour, played with and resolved.
- Learners detect insecurity (they stop believing) and false confidence (they recoil); **genuine confidence is earned** through hard work on scripts and preparation before each session.
- **Sustainable limits:** energy cannot depend on mood. Max ~2–3 hours teaching/day, ~15 hours/week for most people; burnout makes the hat heavier. Recording/workshops mean inhabiting your own theatre plus the student's plus the remote learners' — real mental stress. 4-hour workshops (Cyprus, up to ~60 people, leaving almost conversational) are exhausting (adrenaline until 4–5 a.m., days to come down) and not ideal (shorter, frequent classes better), but prove energy's power: learners won't sustain 4 hours by themselves.
- Never read the script like an instruction manual; your enthusiasm (or grimness) is contagious.

### 2.3 Beyond Public Speaking
- **Address everybody:** each correction/cue should serve all learners and all possible versions of the same learner — one utterance can affirm a rule for one and cue thinking for another, preventatively untying knots.

**Sophophobia (learning anxiety)**
- Fear of *not* learning, and also fear of *learning* (will it all fit in my head? capacity panic, rejecting new info to protect what's learned). Causes picking and choosing what to remember, constant mental recapping.
- Counters (built into courses or ad hoc):
  - Let things go; we're not remembering, we're learning how to *find* things.
  - Nothing is lost — the language exists "out there"; exposure reminds you or resolves doubts.
  - Think one thing at a time to arrive at the sentence; prove you can find it.
  - Use the teacher like a dictionary; structure is the concern, not vocabulary.
- Knowledge is power, and power can be frightening: learners cry or shake not from failure but from *success*; progress feels like "overcoming oneself," traumatic especially for people used to submissive roles. They may need the teacher to give them **permission** to know, and ask to be walked through processes they can already do. Name it delicately ("isn't it interesting what the mind does! Let's dominate this!"); misdiagnosing tension creates more.

**Old habits dying hard**
- Distinguish emotional causes from educational habit (e.g. reciting *hablo, hablas, habla…* vertically to find "we go," lips moving, forgetting the sentence).
- Fix habit by fortifying the independent horizontal process, dismissing irrelevant conjugations, noting that real life needs one form at a time, and asking "why are you running through the others?" — often they can't say.
- Random self-made mnemonic connections (e.g. *llover* = "two lovers in the rain") break contours and divert focus (pronunciation of *ll* vs remembering the word). Ask what they were thinking; gently say: nice, but avoid that *during class*, because it interferes; we focus on elements that teach Spanish. Add justification on repeat; say "during class," not "ever."
- Habit weight varies by culture and prior schooling. Less-educated/mature-returner learners are often *easier* than graduates (fewer entrenched study habits). Misidentifying cause (emotion vs habit) adds confusion.
- **Energy is the universal stopgap**, but unsustainable as the only fix; learners must discipline their own minds or the teacher must keep carrying the class.

### 2.4 The Final Straw
- Minimum requirement: the learner must *try* to participate as cued. If the class consistently breaks down, it's rarely intelligence — it's emotional/personality baggage (tiredness excepted: change the timetable).
- If only "disciplining into thinking" works, the learner may do better with the **audio course** than live. You can end the *relationship*, not their relationship with the method.
- You are not customer service; the collaboration must be fruitful. If they insist on guessing or doing X/Y/Z instead of thinking, the Thinking Method isn't happening.
- **Guessers** often snap out of it under strictness — for a sentence or two; persistent ones relapse. Great learning experience for the teacher, but don't overdo it: burnout risk and negative feelings about teaching (which must stay positive).
- Teacher's first concern is their own energy/stress. Difficult students are still valuable — they magnify every mental trap and improve scripts for everyone; persist as long as reasonable. But deciding "unworkable" takes experience.
- **Berlin anecdote:** in 40–50-person workshops, a woman insisted on writing everything down, aggressively ("you don't know how I learn"). She was the only one unable to produce a target sentence, and the only one interrupting for not-yet-taught material, trying to build a vertical panorama from a horizontal class. Solution: suggest she observe that workshop and try not writing next time. Even workshops can require this in extreme cases.
- Contrast contexts (one-on-one, pairs, workshops) to tell whether the problem is the learner, the script, or the teaching.
- "Anyone within a normal intelligence range can learn a language if they engage and take their time." Failure is often due to **excess** mental energy (doing too much), not too little.
- Tailor to all learners simultaneously (confident/carefree vs nervous/memorising); in workshops, alternate target sentences between learners and give instructions relevant to all — good practice for dividing load between recording student and remote learners.
- Leave no one behind — but the learner must give a little. With experience the mental theatre fills with real learner types and they start shaping the course during writing.

## 3. Course Analyses

- Six openings (~first 15 min) analysed: French, Swahili, Greek, German, Turkish, Arabic — newest first, so the author becomes progressively more self-critical.
- Read alongside *listening*: intonation and phrasing carry much of the meaning; written explanations look clumsier than spoken ones.
- Two aims: (a) opening-specific considerations so new writers can start soon; (b) a demonstration of the *mental wrestling* behind course writing.
- Not everything can be commented on; even these analyses are not exhaustive. Macro (structural) thoughts come early; micro thoughts come with teaching/revising. Nobody should expect to have all of them on a first draft — the author rewrites ~10× more than he writes.
- Comments on live-class effects are not premeditation: you can't control every moment, but internalised method lets you respond coherently.
- A course can work with less thought — but to less effect, for fewer learners, and for shorter-term goals. The magic is in the **content**, not the Q&A system.

### 3.1 French (Introduction to French)

**Framing decisions**
- No intro track: abridged instructions are woven through the first lessons. Rules of engagement are taught like linguistic material — delivered when relevant, not piled up front. Hindsight: the old intro-track dump made little sense, a habit reproduced without critical thought.
- Opening theme: English is half Germanic, half Latin; formal/Latin words (*enquire*) resemble French, informal/Germanic ones (*ask*) don't; learners think of the informal word first, so they must learn to hunt for the Latin one to unlock conversions.
- Opening the course by explaining *how* French will be sourced from English lets the learner relax instead of memorising; "calm and curious" intonation cues the mode. Colourful intonation on "enquire" conveys register even to weaker English speakers.

**Track 1 flow**
- *gâteau* (everyday "cake" in French, a type of cake in English): a valid import only because the meaning is everyday in French; also teaches that a form can move between languages without its exact meaning. *le* is given by the by, without mentioning masculine — no feminine counterpart yet, so that detail would be mental debris (repress the urge to unload everything).
- Spelling of *gâteau* is deliberately not discussed: sound over spelling; no spelling rules yet.
- *café* (coffee / coffee shop) → first solicited application: "the coffee?" → *le café*. Pause-button instruction is given only now, when relevant; remote learners are addressed directly ("break the fourth wall") — more memorable than an intro track. Self-critique: should have said "pause the audio."
- *je veux*: split into *je* + *veux*; *veux* linked to "volunteer" (one who wants) — first etymological hook, building the habit of enjoying such analysis rather than memorising it; also a first sound anchor (starting sound helps the rest roll). The /zh/ sound of *je* exists in English ("pleasure"); spelling confusion resolved by sound.
- *je* vs *veux* have two different vowels; no mouth mechanics given (would overwhelm); focus only on noticing vowels and masking repetition.
- "I want the cake?" — no pre-solicitation: the surprise of a first full sentence is motivating; if the learner freezes, split into "I want" + "the cake."
- *le* correction: student says /le/; the fix (pronounce like *je*) is delayed until after the spelling observation, masking the correction and making it land. Pairing words by spelling/sound will manage the French vowel panorama.
- "I want it" = same word as "the": *le*; placed **before** the verb (*je le veux*). Three restatements (it comes first; before "want"; direct translation) without stating a *rule* ("before the verb" would require defining "verb" prematurely). Deliberate: show word order differs while taking a preliminary step toward the rule.
- *tu veux*: dialectal realisations (/chu/, /tsiu/, Canadian S); the U-quality remark is nestled among dialect notes so learners don't tense up; the /u/ is flagged as unfamiliar (focus) while repetition is masked. One minimal physical tip: lips out (otherwise English speakers would never do it, and it feels silly).
- "You want" is *given*, not solicited: soliciting it would suggest *veux* is generally reusable while verbs change with subject.
- Questions by intonation only for now (real French questions are more complex, later); playful intonation acknowledges the sentence is not especially useful — early on, sentences matter less than the rules producing them.
- "You want it?" with no recap = artificial friction, signalling that autopilot is impossible; the author notes a missed positive reinforcement for *le* before *veux*.
- Recap: words pulled from Latin/French English (*gâteau, café*) plus "unrelated but related" (*veux*/volunteer) form one loose group, soon made inconsequential by conversion rules.
- **-ION rule:** -ION words are Latin and (likely) identical in French; *collection* — same spelling, S instead of English /sh/, N "barely heard but not silent — think about it, finish there." No "nasal" jargon; the N is described to get learners reproducing words without overloading. Repetition of this point gets its own stage and explicitly contradicts the common "silent N" claim — cueing that this course describes language its own way.
- "How might you say…" cues "you don't know it but you can find it." Student's over-strong N on *organisation* is corrected by modelling, not re-explaining — avoid shifting focus.
- Word choice across semantic areas (*tradition, attraction*) demonstrates the rule's breadth.
- "The cake/coffee?" recap is really about the article; *le* is /lə/ not /le/ → overt correction tied to the planned recap, then "but *the collection* is *la*" — a tension contour opened by "but," resolved later.
- Noun defined functionally: "words we can put *the* in front of." "Are considered masculine/feminine" softens the strangeness of gender; **it is the word, not the object, that has gender** — defeats the "how can a chair be female?" objection.
- Masculine → *le*, feminine → *la* (spelt out to encode). *-ION* words are feminine, shown repeated with la (*collection, tradition, confirmation, opinion, organisation, passion*), before the rule is stated.
- *confirmation* revises the nasal N with *con-* (two Ns) — another description (rule later). "The opinion" → *l'opinion*: losing the A of *la* before a vowel actually focuses attention on the A, strengthening the *la* + -ion bond; contraction described then produced (friction).
- "*The passion*?" after *l'organisation* attacks automatism: carrying the L over would produce a gender error.
- Warm-down ends on the conversion rule so learners let material sink rather than try to remember it.

**Track 2 flow**
- Opens with *le gâteau* — same as track 1 — to show how much can now be built. "A word we have in English too" nudges learners away from "cake" and toward *gâteau* (conversion-rule urge).
- Nouns always solicited with their article to encode gender.
- Artificial friction: after "you want," return to "I want the cake" (expected "you want…"), then "I want it" → "Do you want it?" changes both subject and mood at once (could have avoided; the extra load distinguishes automatism from thought and signals growing autonomy).
- "If this *it* means *la baguette*…": a word not yet seen and not an -ION word forces the learner to check the article — installs the gender-checking process. *Baguette* also exists in English, so no translation stop.
- *confirmation* again: two nasalised Ns; held back from rule-making on purpose. Extra motivation offered: not pronouncing the N places vowels more French — something "for free." (Mental-theatre warning: never assume you've explained something just because you're using it.)
- Many more recaps than strictly necessary: builds confidence, teaches that doubts get resolved mid-course (no need to repeat tracks), making learners attentive to clarifications. Rewinding ("I want the confirmation" before "I want it") counters automatism and resolves a small contour.
- **-ATION → -ER rule:** from `-ation` nouns (*confirmation, organisation*) get the verb "to confirm," "to organise" by dropping -ation and adding ER (/e/): *confirmer*. Cue importance: "thousands of sentences." Explicit contrast with plain -ION avoids residual tension. Verb defined by its "to" form, mirroring the noun definition by "the" — both coherent.
- *to confirm* is one idea; two words in English, one in French — again separating idea from language.
- "Don't remember words, learn how to find them."
- **celebrate trap:** learner will extrapolate *celebrater* from *confirmer*; soliciting *célébration* first and repeating the two-step process prevents it and explains *why* it's two steps (the "-ation test" tells you whether conversion is possible). Alternative weaving (saving the rule until verb bases like -ate are known) considered and rejected because it removes that test.
- "I want to celebrate" arrives unexpectedly ("I can't believe I just said that!") — playing with expectation contours.
- Pronunciation weaving (*tu* longer vowel, lips rounded) is deliberate: a small look-away forces recall; physical tips are added incrementally rather than all at once.
- *un peu*: more elaborate sentences, masks repetition; later serves the F→P consonant shift ("few"/"peu") and another N/M syllable (*un*).
- *peu* shares the EU vowel of *veux*; the X of *veux* is still not discussed (no obligation to spell everything).
- Rule applied to *participation → participer*, then to "to get involved": register insight — *participer* sounds everyday in French, whereas "participate" sounds formal in English; learners must hunt for an `-ation` word for an idea they'd normally phrase differently. Reinforces thought-over-word and that French often "sounds more formal" to English ears.
- Flexible word order (*un peu* mid-sentence) is demonstrated to validate alternative learner orders — there is seldom only one way to say something.
- *to visit* via rare "visitation": don't decide too quickly that you can't say something; an upbeat end to lesson 2.

### 3.2 Swahili (Complete Swahili)

**Why this opening works**
- Opens with **onomatopoeia** rather than cognates — proof that "Language Transfer" ≠ cognate conversion. Cognates are used to *reduce load* and raise consciousness, not to define the method; sometimes other options are better.
- Cognates were available ("blouse," "beer," "pizza") but are random international nouns teaching nothing about Swahili's character; noun classes would be too complicated an opening; verb infinitives + conjugation is the right structural start.
- Onomatopoeia is easy despite the term: *kulala* (to sleep) ~ "lull"/"lullaby"; *kucheka* (to laugh) ~ "chuckle" — both only *indirectly* echoic in English. Doesn't matter whether the Swahili etymology is genuinely echoic: offer it as a possibility. Builds curious analysers from minute one (masked repetition, instant deep relatability vs superficial cognates).
- Teaching onomatopoeia is a *means*, not a checklist item; a course writer's job is to spot the language's offers. (*kusafiri*, "to travel"/safari, was the alternative — less engaging, and atypically ends in -I.)

**Track 2**
- Aim foreshadowed: break down how ideas become language (thought/language separation is essential in Swahili immediately, because information order differs so much).
- Easing into "verbs are 'to' words": start from "languages have word types, which behave differently" — a launchpad. Practical writing rule: write what you will *say* to the person, not the point you want to make. Describe word groups by **form** ("verbs take *to*"), not function ("action"), because *to be, to want* aren't actions.
- Swahili verbs take **ku-** instead of *to*: *kulala* = "to sleep," one word vs two. "*ku-*" is never translated as "to"; its *function* is shown instead. Word-count difference (2 vs 1) again separates idea from language.
- Shift focus from *ku-* to *-lala*: echoic explanation (casual intonation → discovery, not memory); penultimate stress introduced with "second-to-last syllable" as a gloss (masked repetition; "stress" glossing "accent").
- *kula* (drop a *la*): artificial friction to test attention and apply the stress rule; weak lead leaves room for error → indirect correction via repeating the rule. "By coincidence" defuses the mnemonic reading.
- Recap (*kulala/kula*) resets the tension generated by demanded concentration and correction.
- Verbs aren't just "to" forms: *I sleep, he sleeps, I slept* all come from the infinitive. English examples demonstrate this; the English comparison then "trails off" — it was there to consolidate "infinitive as launchpad."
- "Which bit **represents** *to*?" — "represents," not "means/is": these aren't translations.
- *-lala* + "information of I" + "information of present": thinking in **information**, not words, makes this smooth. Terminology must be checked ("present" = "I am sleeping," not past/future); many learners don't know it.
- *ni* (I-sound), *na* (present-sound) → *nina*; "sound," not "word" (the word for "I" is *mimi*, deliberately withheld to avoid confusion; will be foreshadowed).
- Two new items at once is unavoidable here, so process them separately *and* together; adding *ni* to *na* gives "I in the present" — application is not postponed. The colloquial shortcut (*na* alone = *nina*) is rejected as a false economy: it would confuse what *na* means and where it goes.
- "Let's run through from the beginning": reframes the pieces as a conjugation process, resets tension, counters memorisation.
- *-lala* is also the imperative, but this is not being taught; phrasing "without the 'to'?" avoids that misreading.
- Student answers *nina-* when asked for "I": teacher insists on splitting into *ni* + *na* so as not to prejudice *nita* (future) / *nili* (past) later. Lighthearted "you're raring to tell me" acknowledges deliberate slowing for remote learners.
- *Ninalala* = first Swahili sentence; repeating the English meaning anchors it semantically and celebrates the achievement.
- *kucheka* resets the contour and re-ties the echoic thread. Alphabet offshoot (Latin script, formerly Arabic script) resets tension, but requires reconstructing base knowledge ("the same alphabet as English") without sounding condescending.
- "Which bit means *to*? Which bit means *laugh*?" — simple successive questions empower a beginner; doing this *always* would irritate, so it's used sparingly.
- Removing *ku-* is asked again here, but framed differently (identifying meanings, not building), which masks the repetition.
- Loose talk ("the 'to' or the 'ku-', no?") keeps focus on function — important because *ku* has other functions and English "to" does too.
- Gradual cue reduction: first "I" and "present" separately, then "two sounds," then *ninacheka* from scratch; feedback again breaks up *nina-* to prevent fossilisation.
- *kutaka*: spelling intonation separates KU | TAKA; only asked "which bit carries *want*" (pace variation); "carries meaning" ≠ "means."
- Open question "how do we get *I want*?" — rare; feedback supplies the highlighted process.
- "I want to sleep" is a whole new structure, framed only by voice ("I want, to sleep").
- *kula* pre-solicited in isolation (not in a sentence) to free cognitive load and supply remote learners with the word; then *ninataka kula*.
- *sasa* (now): repeated syllable, common in Swahili; weave back to *-lala* ("what was *to sleep*?") — surprising familiarity, masked repetition, and a cue to let go of the new word right after giving it (trust the teacher, and later your own thoughts, not to strand you). Defining "syllable" is unnecessary — examples carry it. "Happens quite often" cues that this is **observation, not examinable information** (else learners hold on and hunt for significance). *sasa* as informal greeting added post-recording: word type that isn't a verb and needs no verb process; author would have marvelled at "now" being a time-of-day-proof greeting.
- *ninataka kulala sasa*; *ninataka kula sasa* — *kula* is *not* re-solicited this time (gradual load increase).

**Track 3**
- Lineal recap of four verbs (rare; supplies superficial structure/grounding), emphasising *ku-*; prompts given even when unnecessary help returning learners feel in control.
- "to laugh, sounds a little bit like chuckle?" — intonation cues the similarity is weaker, pre-empting *kuchaka*.
- "Do you remember?" for *kutaka*: signals it's not expected to be memorised (relaxes recall), and blocks the onomatopoeia route. Neutralises the track-2 self-testing doubt: you *have* retained things and you are *not* expected to have them memorised.
- All verbs end in **-A**: noticed "by the by" now; important later (final -A mutates for meaning). Listing the four verbs also resolves the "we know four verbs" tension.
- Re-run the conjugation process step by step (not a target sentence): *ni* → *na* → *nina*, then *kutaka* → *ninataka*. Soliciting the infinitive first sharpens the contrast infinitive/conjugated.
- *sasa* recalled via "repeated syllable like *lala*" — exponential recall boost.
- *ninataka kulala sasa*; flexible order (**sasa ninataka kulala**) — intentionally *not* given with the English equivalent to keep unstitching from English order. The new order's novelty also keeps a recap alert.
- For "I want to eat now," a general "think bit by bit" instruction creates distance from the previous sentence so the learner rebuilds rather than edits; "I want" is solicited first so *sasa* cannot land front-position.
- Heavy cueing after a lightly-cued success is fine: it varies focus and lets remote learners catch up.
- *ni* + *na* mentioned again, now contextualising new info → repetition masked.
- *U* = "you": only "we have the letter U for you" — deliberately *not* "remember it like that" (no artificial hook); most learners won't even notice the coincidence. *una* (you-present) makes the earlier insistence on splitting *nina* obvious.
- Vowel quality affirmation (clear, open /u/; English vowels are weak) is for all learners, not a correction.
- "*kutaka*?" — "just think of the first bit, *ku-*, and the memory follows" (works for unknown reasons).
- "You want to sleep!" — exclamatory intonation prevents it being heard as a question, since questions are solicited by intonation and teachers often *ask* for sentences interrogatively.
- Questions by intonation: the structure is given before the term "questions," so the term lands. "Raise the intonation" is too vague (self-critique: wouldn't record it again).
- Cues reduce again: *sasa* solicited with no repeated-syllable prompt; isolated words let learners spend all energy on recall.
- *unalala* → *unalala?*; the interrogative masks repetition, and the second question method (*je*) is deliberately *not* introduced yet — resist the urge to be "complete" too early.
- *kucheka* also means "to laugh **at**": groundwork laid far ahead of the sentence that needs it (removes future load, cues "don't memorise this now"); also masks repetition of the meaning.
- *unacheka sasa* / *sasa unacheka*, English double translation shows flexibility; familiarity allows momentary focus shifts that keep learners alert.
- Student self-corrects *nina-* → *ni-*: evidence the cues worked; teacher improvises to exploit it before moving on.
- **"ni" also = "me", placed after the "time sound."** Phrased as "after the time sound," not "after *na*," so learners don't over-simplify. *you, present, me, laugh* → *unanicheka*; "at" is unnecessary thanks to the earlier groundwork — relief and trust for learners who noticed; repeated references mean the 4th mention may be the one that internalises it. Learners should deduce from recaps rather than repeat tracks.
- Both orders (*unanicheka sasa / sasa unanicheka*) again.

**Track 4**
- Rewind without cues → novel info: **nataka** shortcut (dropped *ni-*, common in speech) is disclosed pre-emptively so real-world exposure doesn't shake faith in the course; would be omitted if not so common; only first-person singular, so *u-* is retained and over-generalisation blocked via *unataka*.
- "spoken Swahili" clarification delayed a beat for noticeability; the spoken/written distinction is deliberately not opened.
- *kuona* (to see): chosen because it takes "me" as object (review of *-ni-*), it's common, and *ku-ona* has vowel contact for friction. Student says /kuana/: teacher isolates /ona/, then rebuilds *kuona* — teaching *how to resolve* such problems, not just the fix.
- Which bit carries "see"? — *-ona* again via a different route. "I see" solicited with **no cues**, new verb.
- *naona* via the shortcut: spaced repetition leaves a trace for real-world hearing, and masks repetition of the vowel-contact element.
- *uniona* ("you see me") not pre-cued — friction; "how would that be?" with interesting intonation cues extra thought. Then feedback ("*ni-* also *me*, after the time sound") resets the contour. (Recording blooper: "sound time" edit-in.)
- Interrogative repetition of the same sentence, then + *sasa*, exploits the friction moment.
- *nataka kula*: *kula* solicited with no help and inside a sentence — cues that a forgotten word is not a reason to rewind.
- Shortcut/standard both given: the student carried *nataka* over, so the remote learner is gently disentangled; back-and-forth makes the alternative memorable.
- Review sequence (*kucheka → unacheka → unanicheka → unanicheka?*) lowers load as a precursor to the new element.
- **je** question marker: introduced as optional, after intonation is established (two ways of doing one thing is disconcerting, so "if you want to"). New sound taught with "smiley" lips first, more descriptions later; "that's much closer" cues pronunciation as secondary; base pronunciation on natives, not the teacher.
- Mixed English/Swahili solicitation (*je, are you laughing at me?*) shows *je*'s position and that nothing else changes — condensed explanation.
- *je* contextualised post-hoc with English *do* ("Do you see me?"): *do* doesn't mean anything, it signals a question. Deliberately *after* *je* has its own description; the "see, it's not so alien" thread must stay implicit or it patronises.
- Intonation with *je* is optional — an explanation with little practical application, used to internalise *je*; self-critique: no example without intonation was given.
- Not over-cueing: no instruction to use *je*; implied solicitation; both options in feedback if missed.
- "To want?" with no framing = **soliciting confidence** ("you know this"; shifts frame from "being taught" to "this is mine").
- "*Do you see it?*" with non-specific *it* → *it* is omitted (**je unaona?**), followed by contrast with "me" to prevent over-generalisation. The many noun-class "it"s come much later; first show that *it* disappears when there is no noun.
- Marker discussion: languages use markers for questions, attention, address; English "I say" is a marker ("I say, that's a nice jacket"). The example's tension (it doesn't seem comparable) is resolved by **aisee** — English "I say" absorbed into Swahili as a sound/marker, with a Swahili accent (two E's → stress on second-last, *ai-SEE*). Spelling shown to explain assimilation; accent rule recycled at low load (it matters later when *je* = "how" shifts the accent).
- Ending on this discovery keeps the lesson upbeat; "you'd be forgiven for not noticing" frames it as a discovery, sustaining motivation.
- Low-load moment at track end used for a further J-sound note (less friction than English J) — a pattern noticed across all LT courses: pronunciation elaborations cluster at ends of tracks.
- Both versions (*aisee, je unaniona? / aisee, unaniona?*) confirm *je* is never obligatory.

### 3.3 Greek (Complete Greek)

**Why this opening works**
- Instead of whole cognates, it imports **word parts** from English (*peri-, epi-*) — usable for any language pair where shared parts, shifted parts, or parts with shared meaning exist. Builds words from the first minute so Greek's long words never intimidate.

**Track 2**
- Opens with *meno* ("I stay / I'm staying"), called a "word," not a "verb" — saying "verb" would unsettle learners who don't know the term. Because load is low (a bare vocabulary item), the simple-vs-continuous present pairing can be stated explicitly now.
- "You may have noticed two words become one… it's the /o/ that shows *I*": directly addresses the tension of one word ≠ two words; the explanation hangs on *meno* (masked repetition) and encodes it. Self-critique: should have had the student repeat *meno* to secure attention.
- **Word-parts reframe:** *peri-* in *perimeter, period, periphery* = "around/near." *perimeno* = "I wait" ("around stay"). Order: first ask how it *sounds*, not what it means — manipulate form before meaning, introducing the general mechanism; remind of *meno*'s meaning so recall doesn't derail them; "firstly…" makes the awkward solicitation sound like an outtake; the trailing "if we stick *peri-* to *meno*?" nearly builds the word for the learner.
- "Around stay" in exaggerated intonation marks it as *construction*, not translation.
- Build back up (*meno* → *perimeno*) so the learner applies the idea and sees the teacher's recap style — no need to memorise.
- Alternate translations are deliberately partial ("I wait / I'm waiting / I'm around staying"; not "I around stay") to debulk explanations.
- *dhen* (not/don't): introduced as a sound (delta looks like D/triangle) — resets the contour and prepares the later two-TH discussion; "not" glossed so "don't" isn't taken as imperative. The TH of *dhen* = English "then" (not yet contrasted with "think"). "Import the sound over" hints at sound isolation; the letter mention was unnecessary but created a tension contour resolved by that import.
- *dhen meno*, *dhen perimeno*: return in the original order for easier recall. Even a correct *perimeno* doesn't guarantee the learner actually thought *peri-* + *meno* — don't assume; repeat when relevant.
- Omega letter mentioned to separate the -O "I" marker from the verb base (verbs appear in dictionaries in the 1st person), with "you don't need to remember the letters" so learners relax for the interesting history: vowels weren't always written; O adapted from W; Arabic uses the same letter for W and O; Arabic, Greek and Latin scripts share a parent. (Errors admitted: said "/o/" for "O".) Then a clear takeaway: *-o* = I, looks like W.
- **-o → -i for he/she/it:** replacing the isolated -O requires little effort; only sounds are discussed (the /i/ is spelt EI — weaving around spelling). Giving he/she/it + both present translations makes *meni* feel like a jackpot; later, single concrete meanings (*she isn't waiting*) reflect real speech. Both grouping and separating are used across the course.
- Questions: show the English inversion, then relieve it with Greek intonation — but leave the learner to work out "sounding like a question."
- Negative question needs only *dhen* + previous question — masked repetition.
- *me* is like English: low load lets focus go to **position** — before the verb. "Before the verb" is the general rule; "first" happens to be true here and clarifies what the verb is, without risking fossilisation (a later sentence has *me* not first). No "for": *perimeno* = "await." Direct translation is only *partly* direct (only *dhen* is placed oddly) — direct translations target only the element under focus.
- Six minutes in: two subject endings, object position, negation, interrogative, word formation.

**Track 3**
- Recap *meni* without the -ing translations: grouping he/she/it is the aim, not completeness.
- Relaying the conjugation *after* the answer (not leading) shows learners they remember — and that it's fine if they don't, because the course follows up this way.
- "He's not staying": one concrete meaning (not all) mirrors real use; digesting each person separately is as important as grouping them.
- Jump from *dhen meni* to *perimeno* (new verb + new subject) raises load deliberately; a word-formation cue helps.
- *pu* (where): sentence ladders from a common base (*pu meni? → pu perimeni? → pu me perimeni?*) review and extend at once; predictable error is starting with the focal new *me* — fix by asking "so, 'where…'?".
- EI = /i/ for he/she/it (epsilon + yiota): naming letters masks repetition; helps spot verbs in writing (no infinitive in Greek) and use real exposure. Self-critique: forgot to note that /o/ can also be written O — the W/O isolation is weaker without that contrast.
- Swift recap ladder (*meno → perimeno → perimeni → dhen perimeni*) launches the elaboration of *peri-* ("stay around") — "everything is out there in English; we only need to look, listen, think."
- *epi-* ("something like on": episode, epiphany, epicentre): "firstly, how would it sound?" creates curiosity about meaning; *epimeno* = "I insist" ("stay on"). Advanced vocabulary isn't "advanced" — a word's frequency shouldn't gate the course path.
- *yiati* (why) is prepared to split later into *yia* (for/because of) + *ti* (what) — like Spanish *por qué*; useful words + Spanish aside unwind the contour and raise cross-language consciousness.
- **"Finding, not remembering":** "it's not about the word *wait* popping into your mind; it's about 'how did we find that?'" — asking a learner who hasn't remembered to reconstruct models the search, counters paralysis, and invests energy in retrieval.
- *ti perimeni?* ("what is he awaiting?") — literal translation repeated because *yia* was just mentioned; intonation isolates the verbal structure.
- Some sentence batches are admitted to be too demanding (could have pre-solicited *dhen me perimenis*); their point is showing the learner they can take the steps.
- "Showing the learner themselves" can feel gratuitous without intonation marking it as fun, or it reads as delay.

**Track 4**
- "Base verb" used for the first time *to refer back*, not to teach — it describes how *meno* functioned.
- **menis:** add -S to *meni* rather than -IS to *men-*, strengthening S = "you" (later reused for object "you") and remaining true for rarer verb types.
- Informal *you* introduced; the formal *you* is a deliberate gap ("we'll get to it") so learners aren't destabilised by hearing it. Self-critique: should have glossed formal *you* ("to be polite," "you sir/madam").
- "Where do you live/stay?" — pre-solicit *pu* so the learner doesn't hold a conjugation while hunting the question word.
- *pu me perimenis?*: correcting the missing *me* by naming it in the solicitation (vs. the lighter "you're missing some information") — choice depends on contour flow; learners may not remember the exact sentence they were building.
- "*Why* or literally *for what*?" — the literal gloss helps recall via the *experience* of the discovery and reminds that *yiati* is two words.
- *ti perimenis?*: no literal "await" this time (fewer cues, accept errors when nothing new is at stake), then explicit "we're not translating the *for*."
- *kano* (I do/make): "Let's learn a new verb" resets the contour; K pronounced like G for many ears; omega discussion briefly abandons and returns to the verb.
- *kani → kanis*: student stresses the ending; not corrected directly (preserve freedom to find *ti*), just modelled twice. When *ti* still doesn't come, the teacher reconstructs *how* to find it ("think of *why*").
- **Ti kanis?** = "what are you doing?" *and* "how are you?" (answered *kala*, well). The second meaning is revealed *after* the sentence was built with the first — more memorable, lower load. Rote-learned phrases from prior study become moments of realisation.
- Confusion story (Greeks will answer *kala*) makes *tora* (now) contextually important; cues learners to let go of *kala* and keep *tora*. Structural note: this is a move from general panoramic knowledge to detailed specific knowledge — cue that distinction early.
- Student says *ti tora kanis*: diagnosed as holding on to *tora*; "don't worry about *tora* until you get there" — an extension of "think what comes first, then next"; teaches slowing yourself down as a resource.
- *ti kanis tora?* → "what are you up to now"; context, not *tora* alone, carries meaning. Self-critique: giving "make" alongside "do" here risked confusing the verb's two meanings with the phrase's two meanings (habit from Spanish *hacer*).
- *egho* (I, emphatic): pronouns are cheap to add, so saved for a review moment and for a new sound at an opportune contour; "use it if you like, maybe to be emphatic" frames it as a resource (it is obligatory knowledge, but framing it this way keeps learners from memorising).
- Sound teaching: "a soft G that hangs around in the throat" — describe what the sound *does*, not what the body must *do* (tense-up risk). "Soft G" better phrased "soft /g/ as in 'good'" (letter name G is irrelevant).
- Learner finds *ego* themselves — more memorable and promotes seeking connections in exposure; *ego* (big ego = "big I") demonstrates the semantic link, since form-links assumed understood often aren't.
- Emphatic *egho meno* / *meno egho*: flexibility revealed after building (contrast makes it appreciable). English stress on "I'm" cues the emphasis; "I'm **not** insisting" double-stresses *I'm* and *dhen* so learners notice both.
- *egho* is "something you hook on" at either end — isolates the verbal structure from surrounding noise.
- *ksero* (I know): KS is one Greek letter (*ksi*); "you don't need to remember letters… but I'll mention them just in case" — stops learners memorising letter names while keeping them engaged, because letters often serve ulterior pedagogical purposes. KS→X (English X gives the sound mid-word; *ksero* starts with it) and PS→one letter are foreshadowing: the Greek "subjunctive"/closed form always contains KS, PS or S, later reframed as "a type of S." Knowing the destination lets you support ideas hours ahead.
- Sound-vs-letter separation: X gives the same KS sound; prevent "eksero" by noting the KS starts the word.
- *ksero* ladder practising all persons + negation + *me* ("you know me").
- Emphasis on "I" in solicitation must be explained, or the learner attributes it to the subject change.
- *egho* again: "more like a gargling sound but without the heavy friction" — disguised physical instruction; "friction" may panic learners (better: "softer"/"easier"). Pronunciation has special power to paralyse.
- *egho ksero* combines the two new sounds.

### 3.4 German (Complete German) — the German-specific core

**Approach [DE]**
- Like Romance courses use the Latin side of English, German uses the **Germanic side**, giving abundant easy vocabulary while the structure is hacked apart.
- German works through **consonant shifts**, not word endings. Before shifts, start with **"true" cognates** (no shift needed) to learn that German "to"-forms end in **-(E)N**; that ending is then ready to combine with shifts later.
- (Note: Complete German is incomplete and will be continued or rewritten under the author's direction.)

**Track 2 — vocabulary and the first structures**
- Opens by talking about English "actually, before German" — relaxing ("I know English") *and* curious (unexpected); the ideal learning state.
- English = Greek, Arabic, Persian, but mostly **Germanic + Latin**; Latin (via French) = academic/formal, Germanic = intimate/everyday. Forms: long elaborate words Latin (*elaborate*), short punchy words Germanic. So convert the Germanic side to speak German immediately; the general info is then narrowed to the task (cues learners to focus on takeaways, not memorise everything).
- *lernen*: "we just stick **-EN** on *learn*." The A of "learn" is ignored because we work with **sound**; the writer must not let spelling knowledge intrude. "To learn" repeated twice so the "to" is registered without direct focus on the infinitive.
- "*To find*… we'll do the same; how might that sound?" — solicited as an afterthought so learners apply what they know. Expected error /fainden/ → **finden**; the error is allowed to keep load low, then corrected. Cue: mistakes are expected and fine.
- *finden*: German **I** pronounced /i/ — German vowels are uniform, unlike English (builds confidence for reading new words). Self-critique: should have said "is pronounced like," not "we are pronouncing like" (which sounds instance-specific).
- **-EN = "to"** established by joining *lernen/finden*; then **work backwards**: "What is *kommen*?" (learner can derive *to come* by removing -EN). Beginning an explanation often relaxes a stuck learner into knowing the answer.
- -EN expands to -EN/-N (e.g. *ändern*) — a looser comment typical of older courses; later courses avoid such unused debris.
- **ich will** = "I want": new item resets the contour. Linked to **"voluntary"** by *sound* (will = /vill/), *not* spelling — spelling withheld to prevent "I will" confusion and because visual form would dominate. Note the hazard: learners may take the first *syllable* /vo/ of "voluntary" rather than the initial /v/; correct with "'Will' and 'voluntary' both start with /v/." *amateur/amor* parallel (both built from "wanting/love") makes the link meaningful, raises consciousness, and shows how to consume extra info (listen, don't memorise).
- "I want to come?" — asks for the whole then the parts, so the learner sees the whole before dismantling it. "I want to learn?" gives no pre-split: the learner must divide the sentence *and* find *lernen* — increasing autonomy.
- **ich kann** = "I can" solicited as a discovery (*kann* is identical to English, and identical words are paradoxically easier to forget). **Dialect variants**: *ich* as /ick/ (Berlin), /ish/ (south) — mentioned so real-world exposure doesn't shake faith; framed as "you might hear," not to be learned. Correct /ih/ soft vs /kh/ harsh, because German **CH has two pronunciations** that must be distinguished later.
- "I can come" rephrased as "I am **able TO** come": mainly masked repetition of *kommen* = "TO come" (the learner wouldn't yet have an alternative verb form to misuse); phrasing keeps "to" attached to "come."
- **gehen** ("to go") — "it changes a little but you will see the same principle" (the -EN); English heard first, then delay before German, building curiosity. H just **stretches the vowel** — never call it "silent" (H is pronounced in emphatic speech). *ich kann gehen*.
- **nicht** = "not": only one translation given now (not "don't/doesn't"), deliberately, because it will be used with *kann*. *ich* = ICH, *nicht* = N-ICH-T: same sound, spelling split to show it; distance kept from the *ich*-dialects because a separate **hard/soft CH** phenomenon is being built up to.
- **"cannot"** is chosen for introducing negation because *nicht*'s position needs no discussion; "I don't want" would have required it (*Ich nicht will* error).
- "I can't go / I am unable to go / I am not able to go" — varied English phrasings loosen the word↔meaning bond (we translate meaning, not words).
- Learner says /goen/ before *gehen*: shows over-generalisation (English + -EN) and how a wrong form becomes a stepping stone. Teaching practice reveals such processes.
- *Ich will nicht*: "I don't want" = "**I want not**" — the *contrast* with English order is what teaches *nicht*'s position; adding "like 'I can not'" would blur it.
- Pronunciation of *gehen* (/gee-en/, E further back) left until the end of the lesson — pronunciation is deliberately secondary and corrected when not needed again.

**Track 3 — questions, shifts, and load management**
- Opens with the full sentence *ich kann kommen* (proof of progress). **Questions by inversion**, "just as in English": drawing attention is justified because English inversion is the *exception* (normally *do/does*) — exactly the "English exception = German norm" trick used earlier with *cannot*.
- Mix "turning/switching" with "inverting" — formal terms are irrelevant if you know the *doing*; prevents terminological memorisation.
- Rebuild *ich kann gehen* → *kann ich gehen?* rather than carrying the inversion over from *kommen* — the inversion is re-thought each time.
- "We don't want to hear any friction on that **ich**" — pronunciation correction as masked repetition.
- **Undo contractions:** "can't I go?" → "can I not go?" — uncontracted English is closer to German. Old English/formal/uncontracted English are "versions of English" that reconstruct German (*I want not*, *I find it not* later). Established as a habit.
- **Consonant shifts:** "consonants just decided to start being other consonants" (history omitted, classified as history). Purpose framed as deciphering vocabulary you see/hear — curiosity stimulus rather than explanation.
- **P→F:** *hope → hoffen*. "How would you say *to help*?" — no -EN cue, so /helf/ (incomplete, not wrong) → "what do you do to make it *TO help*?" → *helfen*. A **desirable mistake** created by artificial friction; even without the mistake the -EN would be re-affirmed.
- *Ich will helfen* without pre-soliciting *will*; if the learner holds *helfen* while searching *will*, that's used to teach one-thing-at-a-time.
- *Ich kann helfen* → *Kann ich helfen?* → *Ich kann nicht helfen* — the consonant shift is left and re-entered (surprise → memorability → contour).
- Pronunciation: "less /kh/" corrected but not insisted on; relapses tolerated under load; the recording student's *kann* is almost never corrected because most learners don't need it — corrections are prioritised by what matters for the **audience**, not the individual.
- Split "-icht" into two sounds (strong T yes, no /kh/). Since recording, learned that some dialects barely pronounce the T — would have reframed the T as an "extra effort" best not applied to the soft /ih/ (and a dialect-consciousness point).
- *schlafen* ("to sleep"): vowel change (sleep/schlafen) plus P→F; heard in context the shift makes it decipherable. Note the shift may have been forgotten, so the explanation is worded to **re-discover** it.
- */shl/* cluster: learner says /slafen/; correct by affirming the two sounds occur together; "enjoy putting all those sounds together — German gives you lots of these" makes the cluster familiar and enjoyable rather than threatening.
- Student self-conscious about unfamiliar sounds; repeated stumbles on *einschlafen* (ein = "in"; "to in-sleep"): the fix is **slowing down**, extended intervention that forces a reset, "what's the first bit?", "think only about the first bit." Positive feedback when *ich kann nicht* improves with slowness; the lesson keeps this struggle **in the audio** to model how to regain control, though most tedious ruts are edited out. Choose recording students wisely.
- *reifen* (ripen) and *affe* (ape) used at low load to consolidate the shift; useful because they are *unexpected* words you already know. *schiff* (ship) via P→F.
- **einschlafen** opens the thread "German builds vocabulary by adding prepositions/prefixes to base words."

**Track 4 — conjugation, objects, more shifts**
- *ich komme*: remove -N. Choice to start with **first person** rather than "we/they" (which use the infinitive): lower load but risks fossilising the infinitive as the present. First person first protects the infinitive as launchpad.
- *ich lerne*; *ich hoffe* — learner produces *ich hoff* (shift applied straight to English "hope"): correct by exposing the source ("go *to hope*, hoffen") — the *launching from the infinitive* is the point, even though *ich hoff* is colloquially correct. Preventative correction for remote learners.
- **es** = it (will later feed the **T→S** shift). *Ich will es* — no word-order instruction, so learners keep base order unless told otherwise; *ich will's* contraction mentioned as optional ("you might hear") → not load-bearing.
- **Continuous tense:** German's continuous isn't used like English's; most English continuous = German simple present. Alternate translations now, explanation later.
- **"I find it not"** — direct translation + "like old English": saved until the more complex *es* word order. *Ich finde nicht* gets "**I find IT not**" with emphasis so the missing *es* is noticed — "it" is easy to forget, especially when it moves.
- **danke** → **TH→D** (thank→danken); *danke* as "I thank" → working backwards to *danken*; naming "working backwards" teaches the skill and masks repetition. Repeat the direction (English TH → German D) so the rule isn't simplified to "interchangeable."
- **denken** (think): vowel change too — consonant shifts are **orientation/hints for guessing**, not fixed access rules like the Latin-derived endings.
- **baden** vs **bad** (bath): noun conversion with no -EN jolts learners; avoids the term "noun." Bias ("what I expect") leads to dead ends; the lesson is letting go of what you know when it is irrelevant — and trusting that the teacher won't solicit what you cannot derive.
- **Bruder → brother**; **mein → my** (via "mine"); accent note on -er; *Mein Bruder kann kommen* — "can **TO** come" direct translation, stronger now that a conjugated form (*komme*) exists; *kann* being irregular lets "my brother" be the subject without third-person forms.
- Inversion cue via intonation, keeping base order as default; undoing contractions *mein Bruder/kann* avoided to keep that focus.
- **Ding → thing** (T→D): abrupt jump works because learners trust nothing will be solicited without means.
- **denn** ("then") used like "because": the current cue is **too weak** (would revise); sentence *Kann mein Bruder kommen, denn ich kann nicht* recaps and ends with control.
- Final sentence *Ich will gehen, denn ich kann nicht einschlafen* returns to distant material, no de-contraction, ending at high load to give a real sense of achievement. Final learner slip (*einschlafen/schlafen*) corrected through upbeat feedback (accepting *ich kann nicht schlafen*) rather than another pronunciation drill — preserving the lesson's ending.

### 3.5 Turkish (Introduction to Turkish)

**Why this opening works**
- Turkish, like Swahili, is unrelated to English, so the **target language** must inspire the opening.
- Opens on the target language's defining feature: **Turkish is all about suffixes/endings.** Terminology is used briefly to mask repetition and mark importance — but defined immediately and reframed: "suffix is just something you add to the end"; English -S (tree→trees) and -ED (want→wanted) are suffixes; "I'm not fond of the terminology — we'll call them endings." Alien → familiar → confidence.
- Whole English sentences become one Turkish word (presented as *support for the simplification*, not as proof of difficulty). Nothing Turkish has been taught, yet the nature of the language is already clear → curiosity tension to resolve. (Parallel: Arabic opening = "roots and patterns.")
- **-im** ("I'm / I am" as an ending): the first tangible element isn't even a word. Justified because "I am" *is* a word/words in English — the contrast word vs ending *is* the lesson. "Luckily it looks just like *I'm*" — a coincidence, mentioned but not turned into a mnemonic. Chosen over -um (equally frequent) because of that resemblance and the word/ending contrast.
- Explicitly tells the learner what is being done ("we'll pull out words which are the same in English and Turkish") — unusual, but needed because the suffix arrives before any word to attach it to.
- **sosyal** → *sosyalim*; **asosyal** → *asosyalim* (not *antisosyal*, a little friction against autopilot; would have been clearer to first say English "a-social"). Accent falls just before -im (stated as a general rule, not as "the accent doesn't move").
- **aktif** (V→F "here…" — cued as description, not rule); **profesyonel** (-AL→-EL "here"). The -al / -ive / -el sequence deliberately shows there is **no Latin-style conversion rule** in Turkish, silently correcting expectations carried over from Romance courses. "Here" cues description-not-rule; a teacher using the script without understanding might accidentally imply rules.
- *Profesyonelim* = "I am professional" **or** "I am **a** professional" (no indefinite article). Revealed as feedback *after* the sentence = "ah!" moment; better than "profesyonel can be a noun or adjective."
- **-im is not a word; *ben* is**: the word for "I" exists and is used only for **emphasis**, since the information is already in -im ("information," not "word," is included in -im). Position of *ben* mentioned (same as English) not to reinvent the wheel but to **counter over-generalising "Turkish is all endings"** — separate words aren't endings.
- Spelling (*SOSYAL*, *profesyonelim*, YON not ION) is used to make audible differences visible, not to teach spelling.
- **"I am an optimist"** applies the earlier "omit *a*" rule in a context where "I'm optimist" would be impossible — the earlier alternative translation becomes a structural precursor.
- Stress before -im; Turkish stress is **very light** ("Optimistim" with stress at the end is how it sounds when you're anxiously asking "is this correct?"). Learner's stress errors are explained for remote learners, then left largely uncorrected, modelling *why* we don't stop for every slip.
- **-sin** ("you are/you're," informal): introduced explicitly because too long on *-im* sentences would fossilise *-im* where it isn't needed. The "formal you" is only flagged (so real-world exposure doesn't confuse) — self-critique: mentioning formality this early without explaining it may distract; could say "if you want to be polite," "you sir/madam."
- **komik** (funny) interweaves familiar (*aktif/profesyonel*) and novel words with the new suffix.
- **çok** (very; Ç = CH): "very funny" solicited in isolation before the full sentence — separates the ideas, confirms *çok* is a word not an ending. The student recycles *komiksin* prematurely; teacher relays both steps so remote learners see the progression.
- **çok güzel** (great/perfect; *güzel* = beautiful): introduced as a unit, then *güzel* isolated, then "you are very beautiful" — learner must attach *çok* to *güzel* rather than recycle the fixed phrase.
- Student asks "is *çok* a prefix or a separate word?" → "separate; **Turkish has no prefixes, only endings**" and lets *her* recall the opening point. Refusing to answer directly teaches self-resolution.
- Long recap run (profesyonelim → çok profesyonelim → ben çok profesyonelim) with indirect stress corrections ("Profesyo **nel** im" → she self-corrects "ah!") — learners pick up indirect corrections.
- **Vowel harmony caveat:** writing this was hard because -im/-sin only hold after certain vowels; otherwise -um/-sun.

**Track 3 — negation**
- **değil** (not): Ğ with an upside-down point = soft Ğ pronounced like Y ("soft G" term slipped out and is meaningless to English speakers — just say "sounds like Y"). Spoken form **/diil/**: "years of saying değil… saved Turkish speakers some calories." Self-critique: the spoken-form note comes too soon and can blur Ğ-as-Y with değil→diil.
- "I am not" is a *different type* of word from the adjectives used so far; artificial friction = give no help, see whether the learner can work it out alone. Student fails to split ("ben değil"), showing what happens without one-thing-at-a-time; the teacher insists on separating steps: *ben* = "I", then "-im", then *değilim / diilim*.
- Don't correct *değil* → *diil* directly; just model the spoken form; "ben diilim" both stresses *I* and exploits the earlier error (both *ben* and *-im* present at once).
- "-sin **with an N**": preventative correction against assimilating -sin to -sim on audio.
- **Word order:** "you are not funny" = "**funny not you are**" (true direct translation). A semi-direct translation ("funny, you are not") would highlight the key point (*komik* first) but risks "komiksin diil*"; true direct wins here, though too much of it communicates "no rules." Soon an explicit rule is needed: **with *diil*, the ending goes on *diil*.**
- "I am not very funny" is used before the affirmative "I am funny" because the affirmative occasions a phonetic change not yet taught. *Ben* masks repetition.
- Enthusiastic feedback after an unprompted *ben* placement acknowledges learners who misplaced it without commenting on the position.
- **ben / sen** paired — same word type, same behaviour; *sen* feels less like an isolated vocabulary item. Used as artificial structure to recap everything in the guise of practising *sen*.
- "Emphasis/contrast" gets **real-life contexts** ("you are social, but I am not…") so the words become organic — and foreshadows **ama** ("but").
- **Long sentence:** *Komiksin ama çok komik diilim* — break it up: "all mental energy on *you are funny*; don't think about the rest." "Not thinking" is as important as thinking; the drawn-out commentary makes the learner forget the rest. Then re-solicited with *ben/sen* to mask repetition and finally explained: *ben/sen* are optional and should carry over naturally from English vocal emphasis — "this is something you already know how to do" (no need to reinvent the wheel with rules).
- Recap (*aktifim → ben aktifim → ben çok aktifim → ben çok aktif diilim*) is **artificial structure** to show the learner what they know; "-im moves to *diil*" contrast is a precursor to stating suffix placement.
- *Ben optimistim ama sen diilsin*: "I think it's nice with the *I*" — communicates optionality without rules; transfer the *sense* of emphasis from the base language.

**Track 4 — vowel harmony**
- Begins by restating "Turkish is all about endings" as grounding; "we are actually making an effort to make the suffix sound similar to the word's end" — a tension contour, because the *learner* hasn't made that effort: the **writer** chose words whose vowels fit.
- **doktorum**, not /doktorim/: word first, explanation after. "-im and -um are the same thing; /u/ is closer to the /o/ that *doktor* ends in and flows easier." "Feel it yourself: doktorim, doktorum." Self-critique: add "you don't have to move your mouth as much" — explicit feeling.
- Justification stack: flow, helps you hear where words begin/end, and "**languages strive for efficiency — a calorie saver**" across a whole population. Concentrated here (rather than spread out) because the learner's **emotional reaction** to relearning a semantic area must be managed: present vowel harmony as something to appreciate and dominate, not an obstacle.
- **doktorsun** derived by the learner ("-im→-um, so -sin→-sun") — concepts shared, not the ending taught. Course treats -im/-sin as "standard" for launch purposes — artificial, no ending is objectively standard.
- **şampiyon** (champion; Ş = SH, "just like Ç = CH with a tail"): chosen for a final O and low load, so the learner decides by **sound alone**, not by rule. (Two O's in Turkish will complicate the rule later.) Not giving feedback on the final vowel yet protects sound-consciousness.
- Student asks whether she must change the I to U when negating: she is looking for the *ending's* host. Answer: "**This is not grammar. It's pure phonetics — just sound.**" **Vowel harmony** named; it only matters at the moment of adding the suffix; nothing before *diil* affects it.
- **boş** (empty/free): dictionary-style "empty, free" can mislead; frame "empty" as the principal meaning ("I'm free" = "my schedule is empty"), teaching learners not to over-generalise dictionary translations.
- **boşum vs /boşim/**: "try both, see which feels closer"; then the option of referencing *doktorum* (O → -um). Presented as *options*, so the learner doesn't memorise a rule that will soon need complicating. Two methods: compare with a known word, or sound it out.
- Literal "you are empty" keeps the two meanings honest. Student produces *boşumsun* (double ending) then /boşun/ → corrected indirectly (*boşsun*).
- **turist**: a cognate that ends in a different vowel — deliberately chosen *without cues*, so the learner must actively choose the ending (autopilot → "turistim" is correct here, -im). A non-cognate would have distanced them from the -um/-sun endings and made reversion automatic.
- **yorgun** (tired): interweaving Turkic words with loanwords makes them feel special and desirable. *Yorgunum → çok yorgunum → çok yorgun diilim* with no cues, measuring independence.
- Semi-direct vs true direct translations are equivalent if intonation separates "very tired" from "I am not" so the ending lands on *diil*.
- Contrast sentences: *Ben* is effectively obligatory for "but I am not"; *sen* optional for "you are very tired" — cued lightly, not taught as rules; it emerges through use.
- **Final check sentence** ("I am free but I am tired") tests for the over-generalisation "ama ⇒ use ben/sen": learner momentarily switches to -sun after *ama* — proof that these micro-considerations really do leave marks.

### 3.6 Arabic (Introduction to Arabic) — kept for the mechanisms it demonstrates

**Why this opening works (and breaks the rules)**
- General principle: begin with **common ideas** (not "common language") — "I want…", "I have to…", "I can…" — which leads into the verb system. Arabic proves that no one can *tell* you how to begin; only how to think about it.
- Arabic's umbrella concepts are **roots and patterns**, which run deeper than the verb system. Root sets = 3 sounds (usually) tied to a meaning: /s/f/r/ travel (safari), /sh/r/b/ drinking (sherbet, syrup), /s/l/m/ peace/submission (islam, muslim, peace, unscathed, safety). Patterns slot the roots in to make specific meanings.
- The verb system is a **branch** of the root/pattern tree, so the Introduction teaches roots/patterns and **avoids verbs almost entirely**. Enabled by Arabic features: an **adjective form used with verbal meaning**, and **possession via a preposition** (no verb "have"). This allows present/past/future-style sentences without the verb structure, laying groundwork for the (unrecorded) Complete course.
- Self-critique: recorded in 2014 as the first *scripted* course; it opens with quite a monologue that "could or should" have been divided up and interwoven with more interaction.

**Track 2**
- "Before we start to learn Arabic…" relaxes the learner and frames the opening as big-picture, not memorisation.
- **Dialect vs language is political** ("the only difference is an army and a navy"); there are only dialects, some of which we call languages. "A dialect of English" implies lineage; "an English dialect" is more accurate. Removes the hierarchy so variation is the norm — crucial because MSA is an artificial, school-learned, native-speaker-less standard.
- **Romance parallel:** French/Portuguese/Spanish/Romanian are Latin dialects with national names; Moroccan/Iraqi Arabic differ more than Spanish and Portuguese yet are all called "Arabic." We are already familiar with this situation (north vs south of the Mediterranean). Turns the tables: you don't have to learn lots of Arabics; we *call* lots of languages "Arabic." Builds on itself: roots/patterns later let you access all dialects.
- **MSA / Fus-ha ≈ Latin** (school language for work, news, religion). Down-sells MSA *because* the course is Egyptian, not the reverse. Traditional courses taught MSA for business reasons; for culture/everyday communication it isn't the best start. Introduction is deliberately not titled "Egyptian Arabic" so learners hear this before deciding.
- **Cairene/Egyptian Arabic** chosen: widely recognised due to Egypt's popular culture; MSA is also more complicated than most dialects (more buy-in). Cairo's Arabic is "standard Egyptian" though Alexandria differs — glossed over without overwhelming.
- Learning Egyptian via roots/patterns lets you **work out other dialects**; you need not preserve the accent and may adopt a **pan-Arabic mix** as natives do — relieves tension about the dialect situation. Ungrammatical "Arabics" is used deliberately to reinforce the point.
- "**Personality** of Arabic" signals generality across all dialects; Arabic is **Semitic** (Hebrew, Aramaic) — the detail makes it feel like discovery.
- **habibi:** strip the vowels → /h/b/b/ in that exact order = liking/loving; then *hobb* (love), *mahboub* (popular) — with *ma-* flagged as "pattern, not root." Self-critique: better to start with a root of three *different* sounds and avoid mentioning patterns until they get attention; "we'll see it later" comments are bandaids for imperfect design.
- **/s/f/r/:** *safari* (arrived in English **via Swahili**), *sifaara* (embassy). **/sh/r/b/:** *sherbet*, *syrup* (sounds changed); Turkish /şarap/ wine helps the learner guess drinking — using the learner's own background, and resolving the tension of a wordless opening.
- Normally three roots, sometimes two or four, rarely five — the anomalies mask repetition of "usually three."
- **Big admitted flaw:** the whole track produces no Arabic sentence, no solicited production; the "internal dialogue" can't be followed. A remake would build the framework gradually instead of monologuing. The experiment proved every course needn't open the same way, but the "standard way" may still be best.

**Track 3**
- Recap of the three roots + new *sifaara* (novelty masks repetition). Roots + patterns let you **guess unknown words and experiment with forming words**.
- Self-critique: mentioning patterns *now* is offloading; the "roots and patterns" payoff is too far away to feel relevant — a tension contour that takes too long to resolve.
- Establishes the habit: strip vowels to find roots, in every new word (chiefly to mask repetition at this stage).
- **gayy** ("coming," deliberately not "come," keeping the word type ambiguous): notice the odd double Y; both must be pronounced. Two /y/ echoes the two /b/ of *habibi* (where they're separated; here together).
- **Root order never changes:** different order = different root/meaning. (Point made here mainly to mask repetition; a full demonstration would use a same-sounds-different-order set.)
- **ana** = I. Arabic has no word for am/is/are (present): "I'm coming" = "I coming." Self-critique: relating am/is/are all at once is premature; just say "no word for 'am'" for now.
- *Ana gayy*; *ana* is optional when the subject is obvious ("where are you?" → "coming!") — masking repetition by still making them think about omitting *ana*.
- **Word types:** avoiding grammatical terminology, but verbs must be defined because word groups behave differently. Verb = a "to" word; "doing word" is a poor definition ("to be" does nothing); "is" comes from "to be" (you don't say "he be") — interesting and memorable, but badly placed given the earlier "no is/am/are" comments (risk of false connection).
- Verbs = the "to" form and everything that comes from it. And Arabic lets us say a lot without the verb structure. Self-critique: "the impatient Mihalis of 2014" said too much too soon — better to work through target sentences and only then marvel at verb-free Arabic; impatience flourishes when you're absent from the learner's mental theatre.
- Reason 1 to say much without verbs: **"to be" in the present is (mostly) invisible**. "Mostly/generally" subtly prevents over-generalisation without creating a nagging tension. Uncontracted "I am coming" (not "I'm coming") exposes the missing *am*.
- Reason 2: words like **gayy are adjectives**, not verbs — "describing words" (yellow, big, sad), coming from verbs and fulfilling verbal meaning. Feminine: *ana gayya* (-A). Analogy: "the coming car" beside "the yellow car" / "the big car" — *gayy* fits the adjective slot beautifully (a lucky fit; "the seeing car" wouldn't). Boils down to: these words behave like adjectives, "which is consequently much more simple" — resets tension.
- Soliciting the "other gender" ("and if you were female?") is a simple workaround when the recording student can't model it naturally.
- **Names as vocabulary:** Arabic names carry meanings; *Kariim* = generous → *ana kariim* ("I'm generous," also "I'm Kariim" — the ambiguity is explicitly enjoyed); feminine *ana karima* (learner naturally shortens the long /ii/ — affirmed in feedback; the shortening is a prelude to the total disappearance of the short /i/ in adjective-verbs + -A).
- *Amiin* (honest; Turkish *emin* "sure"): meanings shift between languages, as "are you sure?" shows even in English. Self-critique: the edit-in is a bit contrived and badly placed.
- "I'm honest" → *ana amiin* → *ana amina*. Saying the "obvious" cues that not all names work as adjectives (confirmed later). **Male recording student was useful:** start masculine, add -A; starting feminine would require removing -A and *lengthening* the /i/ with no intuitive justification. A feminine-first course isn't impossible — feminine/plural both drop the final /i/ of adjective-verbs (*shaayif* / *shaayfa* / *shaayfiin*) — the masculine could be reframed as the anomaly. Writers should wrestle with exactly such framings; wild ideas are worth pursuing even if they lead nowhere.
- Names have masculine/feminine forms (*Kariim/Karima*, *Amiin/Amina*); weaving returns names → adjectives → feminine → feminine names.
- Not all names are currently used as adjectives; still, asking what names mean builds your root bank (softens the disappointment with a useful minimum).
- Final recap of *gayy* + congratulating the double Y re-ties everything to the root idea.
- **Lesson of the section:** great weaving, but too compact — the temptation to "unfold" everything at once. First drafts are still compact today; repeated read-throughs spread two pages over half a course.

## 4. The Nitty Gritty

### 4.1 Grammar/Structure Panoramas (preparation)
- Before writing, build **panoramas**: visual representations of everything the target language's grammar does. Typically **vertical** tables (e.g. all verb forms by tense/mood, with usage notes).
- **Horizontal reading** across vertical tables is where the discoveries happen:
  - Spanish: every "they" form uses **-N** (*-an, -ieron, -aban*); every "we" uses *-mos*; every "you" uses *-S*. Teach the idea, reaffirm it across endings.
  - Conclusion: verb endings are usually **two parts — a time marker + a person marker**, not one sound. Learners can then derive forms (*-aba-* + they → *-aban*).
  - Textbooks usually group only vertically and miss this coherence.
- Also look for **similarities of thought**, not just form: word-stress movement (penultimate vs final vs antepenultimate) is influential in course design, especially if nouns manipulate stress differently — weave together or keep apart. Stress sits beyond a word's form, so it's easily missed without inhabiting the learner.
- **Build a panorama for every area, however simple** (e.g. nouns). English nouns "just pluralise with -S," yet also take possessive -S ("friend's"/"friends'" — interference with plural), irregulars (mice, lice, octopi, sheep, series), and behaviours ("20 pounds **is** not enough"). Unexpected items surface later — leave space.
- The representation is personal; it need not be neat. The author covers a wall with paper and arrows; messy is fine because panoramas log discoveries made *while* making them. ("What takes two minutes here can take two days through writing.")
- Look for **everything**, not just what the base language does: Turkish evidentials (witnessed vs reported), inclusive/exclusive "we", feminine "you", etc. Otherwise huge swathes stay uncovered.
- **Also map the base language's perspective:** if English has a continuous present and the target only has one present form, the continuous *idea* must appear in the panorama, so the course doesn't forget to teach that the simple form covers it.
- **Swahili panorama example** (the "who / when / to who" arrows): subject (+ negative persons), tense sounds (+ negative tenses), only object sounds that change. Discoveries made there:
  - Start teaching the negative with the **future** (its *-ta-* needn't change in the negative).
  - **Save first person last** in the negative (*ni* → *si* is irregular).
  - Moving from infinitive to verb requires **two mental movements**: person + time.
- **Don't fossilise your own analytical descriptions:**
  - The object sound is the "third sound" in the writer's list — but teach "**after the time sound**," because sentence-building thinks first-then-next, not by numbering.
  - Check possible problems: in the negative present there *is* no time sound — but **absence is still a type of presence** mentally; you can still place the object "after" the (omitted) time sound.
  - Dormant hypotheses are worth keeping: *-ku-* as object "you" makes *nilikuona* end like the infinitive *kuona*; the "maybe it's the infinitive + omitted you" reframe was doubtful and led nowhere, but the thought had to be had.
- **Note everything, even what you won't teach** — unpromising irregularities confirm bigger ideas. Future *-ta-* (sometimes *-taka-*) later confirmed *-ta-* ← *kutaka* ("to want"), just as English "will" comes from the Germanic word for "want" (language-consciousness payoff + tension contour).
- **Published grammar books contain errors** (including from the author's own negative transfer and simplification). Don't trust print; compare sources, cross-check with exposure, consult native speakers. Your own critical thinking is the only real authority.
- Course writing isn't the goal of this stage: the panoramas are for **your** thinking and for resolving doubts before teaching decisions. Inspiration comes both during panorama-making and during weaving.

#### 4.1.1 Vocabulary Panoramas
- Lists not of "essential/common" words (though common words are preferred when possible), but of words that **teach the language's nature**: vocabulary formation, structural/grammatical/phonetic issues.
- Having these early lets vocabulary weaving happen alongside grammar from the start (e.g. Greek *meno / perimeno / epimeno* woven into present tense + negation). Much vocabulary discovery still happens during writing and is woven in later.
- **Indo-European (IE) patterns** (from the Spanish, Italian, French, German, English, Greek courses):
  - **Preposition + base**: English "up" (think *up*, come *up*, run *up* a bill, eat *up*, take *up*) / "take" (take off, on, in, out, out on, over); Latin *de-* (decipher = "from zero," demonstrate = "from monster/omen," deport = "from door," demand = "from hand," desist = "from stand"); base *-sist* (insist = stand on, exist = stand out, resist = stand again, persist = stand through, assist = stand to, consist = stand with). Same logic for nouns (*insistence*) and adjectives (*insistent*).
  - These panoramas feed grammatical points, provide curiosity/load-resetting dissections (exist = "stand out"; *decipher* = "from zero," combining Latin *de* + Arabic *sifr*), and access functional prefixes (*de* = from, *con* = with in Spanish).
  - Golden words: Greek *symperiferomai* = Latin-English "comport oneself": *syn/m* = *con/m* ("with"), *peri* ("around," absent in English), *feromai* = "port oneself" (P→F shift) — teaches shifts and double-preposition verbs at once. "Demonstrate" (*-mon-* = showing: monitor, admonish, money) → Spanish *demostrar* → *mostrar* ("to show"), which supports **indirect object pronouns** (showing *to* somebody) and the O→UE stress change. Keep lists of verbs for indirect objects and reflexives.
  - **Consonant shifts** (sounds made in similar mouth positions; may not "sound" alike). English→German: P→F (help/helfen, hope/hoffen, ripen/reifen, ape/Affe, ship/Schiff); English→Romance: F→P (father/padre-père-padre, for/por-pour-per, feather/pluma-plume-piuma, foot/pie-pied-piede, fable→parler/parlare); F→Spanish silent H (fable→hablar, fervour→hervir, fraternal→hermano, fugitive→huir, fig→higo); English→German T→S (water/Wasser, what/was, eat/essen, let/lassen, street/Strasse); CH /k/→Greek /kh/ (chaos/khaos, chrome/khroma, character/kharaktiras, chronology/khronos, chamomile "ground apple," chameleon "ground lion").
  - Also **within** the target language: *hierro* (iron) / *ferrocarril* ("ironway"/railway); *hermano* / *fraternal*; *fumar* / *humo*; *fervor* / *hervir*; Spanish LL/CL/PL (*llave* / *clave*, *lluvia* / *pluviosidad* — "clef" links to English).
  - Unlikely shifts are rewarding: "file" → *hilo* (thread), confirmed by English "in single file" = "in single thread" (would be a prime tension-contour example; the author only thought of it now).
  - **Suffixes:** -ate/-ative/-ation; -ance/-ation; -ive/-al. For Romance-from-English, **don't teach what word class an ending produces** — it's identical in base and target (reinventing the wheel, mental debris). Do teach it if the base is Turkish/Mandarin etc. Instead, use endings to import "convertible vocabulary" and direct focus to pronunciation, stress, consciousness. (Convertible vocabulary is the most visible — but not defining — feature of the method.)
    - -ive → Spanish *-ivo*; -al → Italian *-ale*.
  - Noun gender change → new noun (*el capital* finance / *la capital* city; *el cura* priest / *la cura* cure); word-class jumps (*poder* / *el poder*; *paro* / *el paro*; *hecho* / *el hecho*); both (*parado* / *la parada*; *comido* / *la comida*; *ido* / *la ida*); stress shift (Greek *pote* "never" / *pote* "when"; *alla* "others" / *alla* "but"; English *permit* noun/verb); same form for different classes (the "-ing" of interesting/amazing vs swimming/sewing vs thing/spring — misidentification harms comprehension and later learning); **diminutives/augmentatives** (*bocadillo* = "little mouthful" but a sandwich; *pesadilla* = "little heavy" → nightmare; *lima* lime / *limón* = "big lime" — a real hybrid; *-ito, -azo, -ete, -ín*). Interweave literal/figurative size use.
- **Swahili (Bantu):** noun classes create new nouns from a base (*kiti* "thing" = ki- man-made + -ti entity; *mti* "tree" = m- natural; *rafiki* friend → *urafiki* friendship = "friendness"). Verb → noun: *kupenda* ("to love") → strip *ku-* → *penda* → add noun-class prefix *u-* → change final vowel to O → *upendo* ("love"); *kucheka* → *kicheko* ("laughter") (the *ki-* class doesn't fit well, as with *kichwa* "head" — frame as a decoding aid or a conversion rule depending on reliability). **Prepositional verb suffixes:** *kupiga* hit → *kupigwa* be hit → *kupigia* hit to/at (*kupigia simu* "to phone") → *kupigana* hit each other/fight; combined *kupigiwa simu* "to be phoned by." These are grammatical but create "vocabulary" from an English perspective.
- **Order of panorama-making:** usually grammar first, then vocabulary (so you know which words the structure needs); but vocabulary can drive structure too (Swahili echoic verbs inspired the opening; English homophones inspired Introduction to English; Arabic vocabulary defined the whole course).
- **Arabic (Semitic):** roots × patterns = vocabulary and grammar inseparable. /h/b/b/ love, /s/f/r/ travel, /s/l/m/ peace/submission. Pattern *ma-1-2-a-3* = place: /k/t/b/ writing → *maktab* (desk/office); /T/b/kh/ cooking → *maTbakh* (kitchen). Modifications of patterns rather than new patterns: add T → "cause to" (*badris* "I study" → *bat'dris* "I teach"); doubled roots, flexible-root sound changes, prefixes/suffixes.
- **Turkish (Turkic):** -tir- = "cause to" (*gelmek* "to come" → *getirmek* "to bring" — the L is repressed); -ş- = "one another" (*anlamak* "to understand" → *anlaşmak* "to agree"). Infix-spotting shortens long verbs and lets learners guess meanings.
- **Unsystematisable gold** deserves a note anyway: *ventana* ("window" as "wind-eye," *viento* wind, *vent-/vient-* recap); Arabic *baab* (door) and Babylon ("gate of god"); Romance numbers hidden in month names (December/dec=ten/*diez*, November/nov=nine/*nueve*, October/oct=eight/*ocho*, plus the ten-month calendar); days of the week (Thursday = Thor's day, German *Donnerstag* "thunder's day" with TH→D, Spanish *jueves* = Jupiter; *lunes/luna*, *martes/Mars*, *miércoles/Mercury*, *viernes/Venus*). Keep free corners on both panorama types.
- **Where connections come from:** your own imagination. Over 90% of suspected connections are confirmed by etymology; ~5% remain obscure (the most exciting); ~5% are wrong. Check suspicions in etymological references. The habit grows exponentially easier.

### 4.2 Putting Pen to Paper
- Writing is always an **exercise**; most drafts will be trashed or become unrecognisable. What matters is where the writing leads.
- Writing reveals new things about the language; expect backtracking and pre-inserting earlier.
- Sometimes one beginning is clearly best; sometimes several are compelling — write them all. Dead ends aren't wasted if you learn.
- **Content principles for a beginning:**
  - Teach **overarching, generally applicable concepts** that open doors ("weaving around" specifics: Arabic avoids verbs entirely; Swahili avoids the negative; Turkish avoids the interrogative) — until structural groundwork makes them easy.
  - **Build sentences, and quickly** — avoid long vocabulary-conversion or monologue intros (the main fix for an Arabic remake). Learner production is what teaches them to read your cues.
  - General preference for **common language/structure**: present before past/future, first person singular before others — but these are *light* preferences overridden by structural considerations.
  - Prefer **few explanations with varied application**; save specific/contrived material.
- **Artificial friction** must still be present so the course never feels autopilot-able. The first lesson teaches the *method*, not just language. Introduction tracks tend to go "in one ear and out the other," so they are references; the teacher must cue the expectations throughout.
- **Greek opening** (present conjugation + negative + infinitive, woven with *meno/peri-/epi-*) vs a hypothetical **noun-first** path (*ekho* "I have" to build noun-focused sentences, later expanding into verbs/perfect) — problems exist in any beginning (here: neuter-only nouns because of case); they are also opportunities. Try both versions. Some languages "slap you back" repeatedly (objects needed for complete sentences; phonetic difficulty as in French vs Spanish); others seem to "guide" you. Once a sturdy generally-applicable beginning is in place, the language constrains you less.
- Course writing continues away from the desk — the subconscious throws ideas at you in the street; keep notes.

### 4.3 Working with Native Speakers
- Confer with natives even if you are native yourself: to catch your dialect/idiolect and things you don't notice about your own language.
- **Don't ask for rules or confirmation of your rules** (except with unusually language-conscious people). Use natives as **blind experiments**: withhold your hypothesis and give situational contexts. "Would you use the present here?" invites unreliable answers — conscious introspection destroys native intuition.
  - Instead: "Your friend says 'see you next week'; you answer 'see you *tomorrow*' reminding them of your party — how would you say that?" Ask several natives with slightly different contexts.
- **Difficulties:**
  - *Imagined/unimagined context:* proofers judge sentences by a context in their head, or miss the context in the audio (they aren't paying attention like learners). Especially common with emphatic sentences. If a proofer's imagination is locked in, ask the same thing in a different context (masking repetition).
  - *Assuming knowledge:* most natives have little conscious knowledge of their language. Usually that's exactly what you exploit (subconscious knowledge via situational prompts). Sometimes you must *teach* them first (Arabic roots/patterns), because such errors still produce perfect sentences and aren't self-evident.
  - *Writer bias:* people seek confirmation and you may "hear" the answer you want; volunteers may please you. Withholding the hypothesis counters both. Be passionately concerned with **truth**, not being right — play devil's advocate against your own hypotheses.
- Expect the work to be tricky, frustrating and treacherous; stay patient and appreciative. Treasure consistent proofers and invest in them; proofing is also a rewarding exchange (they discover their own language and their non-standard idiolects).

## 5. The Competition and the Direction Relationship

- Direct tone is deliberate: the author has been "tortured" by **anti-volunteers** — average people who promise help and vanish, costing time and energy. A broken promise is not neutral. Real volunteers made the project possible; the system must put the initial investment on the new writer.

### 5.1 The Competition
- Ongoing competition (opening with this book) to select up to five new writers/teams, directed by the author, for: **Mandarin**, two Intro courses made Complete, one language from the top-ten vote, and a "mystery" language chosen purely on merit (any language).
- Submit the **first five tracks** (or five continuation tracks) of your chosen course:
  - Written tracks **with justifications** (like Part 3), focusing on how the opening sets up the whole course — expose your plan.
  - **Recorded with a test student and edited** (e.g. Audacity). If the base language isn't English, provide literal translations of explanations.
  - A **rough plan and/or grammatical panoramas** of the whole course with expected connections and supporting justifications.
- Tracks should be treated as final, scrutinised, edited and practised — a demonstration of talent, research ability, self-motivation.

### 5.2 The Direction Relationship
**Money and more**
- Collaboration lays the foundation of a career: discounted private classes during training; higher rates once "fully formed" and the course is online; access to LT's untapped premium gigs (in-company, etc.).
- Target: a good full-time wage on **2–3 teaching hours/day**. The author believes LT teachers should charge like high-end lawyers/consultants — no shame in exclusive rates when the material is free and the rates let teachers give limited, all-in classes and keep improving free material.
- **Hours must be capped** so energy/preparation stay high; half-baked classes disserve the method and the exclusive rates. Teaching is also an ongoing experiment feeding the course; teachers must not get rusty (it's easy to forget and lose touch with learners). The cap keeps teachers off autopilot and improving.
- Optional extra: free visual publications/PDFs/hard copies gifted with workshop donations.

**Our relationship — writer responsibilities**
- Course writing as **main activity and priority** (not a side project; other jobs/hobbies fine, but most cognitive energy here).
- Commit to realistic weekly hours — minimum **15 h/week** of writing and teaching practice; changes discussed.
- **Welcome critique**: the director's job is to find holes; come thirsty for insight, not for praise.
- **Act on advice**; don't resubmit until previous critiques are addressed; be proactive and organised.
- **Stay in communication**; communicate breaks/deadlines; disappearances are not tolerated.
- **Source native-speaker volunteers** to cross-check both languages.
- Help create at least minimal media about the experience.
- **Generate and nurture your own motivation** — know why you're making the course and be ready to find new reasons.
- **Respect the project**: every hour of direction is funded by LT users — intend to at least complete an Introduction (≈ one third of a Complete course).

**Fail-safes**
- **The course belongs to LT**: it contains the author's ideas and funded direction time; it cannot be withheld from free release or commercialised, and if a writer leaves, the material remains for future writers.
- **Batches of ten tracks**: pre-planning makes this safe; if a writer quits, the course can continue from where it stopped, and a departing writer should signal early and finish the current batch.
- Final note: the project should outgrow the author, but not through more unsustainable sacrifice — the onus is now on the new writer.
